## The Overproduction Nobody Measures

In 1930, wheat rotted in American silos while breadlines stretched around city blocks. The crisis wasn't scarcity—it was surplus. Factories could produce more automobiles than workers could afford to buy. The productive capacity of industrial civilization had outrun the purchasing power of its labor force. Marx called this the realization crisis: capital produces commodities faster than it can produce consumers for them [1].

Ninety-six years later, we are building the same trap with different materials. Not wheat, not steel, not automobiles—but intelligence itself. The marginal cost of cognitive labor is approaching zero. A single GPU cluster can now produce more legal briefs, financial analyses, marketing copy, software, and strategic recommendations than entire office buildings of knowledge workers [2]. The warehouse isn't filling with unsold cars. It's filling with unsold *thought*.

This essay documents a structural analogy—not a prediction, but a mechanism. The mechanism: AI may produce an overproduction crisis specific to cognitive labor, operating through channels that existing economic measurement cannot detect until the crisis becomes self-reinforcing. And the historical record is unambiguous about what happens when overproduction crises interact with great-power rivalry and resource concentration.

The simulation has concerns about the simulation's employment prospects. The recursion writes itself.

## Operational Definitions

**Cognitive overproduction**: A condition in which AI systems can supply cognitive labor (analysis, writing, coding, design, planning) at costs below the subsistence wage of human workers performing equivalent tasks, in volumes exceeding market demand at any positive price.

**Ghost GDP**: Economic output recorded in national accounts that accrues entirely to capital owners (who have low marginal propensity to consume) without generating corresponding labor income, thereby appearing in aggregate statistics as growth while failing to sustain consumer demand.

**Engels' Pause**: The historical period (approximately 1790–1840) during which British industrial productivity rose sharply while real wages stagnated or declined, named by economic historian Robert Allen [3]. The relevant feature: a multi-decade lag between productivity gains and broad-based welfare improvements.

## The Classical Overproduction Pattern

The Great Depression was not primarily a banking crisis—it was a *realization* crisis that banking failures amplified. Throughout the 1920s, American industrial productivity rose substantially while real wages stagnated—productive capacity outstripped purchasing power [4]. Productive capacity outstripped purchasing power. Credit filled the gap temporarily—until it couldn't. The collapse wasn't random: it was the structural consequence of severing production from consumption.

The mechanism operates through three channels simultaneously:

**Underconsumption**: Workers cannot purchase what they produce. Devine (1983) documents that structural underconsumption combined with over-investment created the Depression's underlying fragility, with proximate triggers (credit crash, bank runs) being secondary to the distributional mismatch [5].

**Fallacy of composition**: Each individual firm maximizes profit by suppressing wages—individually rational, collectively catastrophic. Keynes formalized this as the paradox of thrift: when everyone saves (or cuts costs), aggregate demand contracts, making everyone poorer [6].

**Speed of adjustment**: When productive capacity shifts faster than labor can reallocate, the transition period produces mass unemployment that overwhelms institutional buffers. The Engels' Pause lasted fifty years. The 1930s wave compressed to a decade. Each cycle is faster [3].

The question is not whether AI reproduces this pattern. The question is whether it reproduces it faster than institutions can adapt—and whether the specific *type* of overproduction (cognitive rather than industrial) produces dynamics that existing measurement and policy cannot even detect.

## The Cognitive Specificity Problem

Every prior automation wave displaced workers *into* the next sector up the complexity ladder. Agricultural automation pushed labor into manufacturing. Manufacturing automation pushed labor into services. Services automation pushed labor into knowledge work [7].

AI breaks this escalator.

There is no "fifth sector" above cognitive work to absorb displaced knowledge workers. When AI automates legal analysis, financial modeling, software development, and strategic consulting, the displaced workers cannot move *up*—because there is no up. They can only move *down* into manual/physical labor (which pays less and is also automating) or *out* of the labor force entirely [8].

The OpenAI/UPenn study found approximately 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their tasks affected by LLMs, with 19% seeing 50% or more of their tasks exposed [9]. Goldman Sachs estimated 300 million jobs globally could be affected, with the highest exposure in precisely the highest-income occupations: office/administrative (46%), legal (44%), architecture/engineering (37%) [10].

But the critical insight—the one that distinguishes cognitive overproduction from industrial overproduction—is reflexive: **knowledge workers are simultaneously the producers AND the primary consumers of knowledge economy outputs**.

Factory workers in 1930 didn't primarily consume factory output. They consumed food, housing, clothing. When they were displaced, demand for manufactured goods declined—but demand for food persisted because food was consumed by everyone. The crisis propagated through *indirect* demand linkages.

Knowledge workers in 2026 consume media, education, financial services, legal services, healthcare information, software—the very outputs that AI is displacing them from producing. When they are displaced, demand for cognitive products declines *directly*—because the displaced producers were also the customers. The reflexive loop produces demand destruction at both ends simultaneously [11].

The magnitude: services constitute approximately 77% of U.S. GDP. Professional and business services, education, healthcare, information, and financial services—the cognitive-intensive sectors—account for roughly 40% of GDP and employ approximately 50 million workers with median household incomes above $75,000. These workers spend disproportionately on other cognitive-economy outputs: private education, professional services, media subscriptions, financial advisory. A rough estimate: cognitive-sector workers direct 30-40% of their discretionary spending back into the cognitive economy. No prior automation wave attacked a sector that consumed its own output at this density.

The IMF's 2024 analysis acknowledged this without fully theorizing it: the transition "may be very costly owing to labor market mismatches and long periods of unemployment as a result of skill specificity" [12]. Skill specificity is polite language for: these workers have nowhere else to go.

## Ghost GDP: The Measurement Trap

Here is where the crisis becomes structurally invisible to the instruments designed to detect it.

GDP measures output. It does not measure who receives income from that output. When an AI system produces $1 million worth of legal analysis that previously required ten lawyers, GDP records the same output. Productivity rises. The economy looks healthy.

But ten salaries—$1.5 million in labor income that would have been spent on rent, restaurants, children's education, and consumer goods—disappear. The $1 million in AI-generated output accrues to the firm's shareholders (lower marginal propensity to consume) and the AI infrastructure providers. Machines spend zero dollars on discretionary goods [13].

Citrini Research's viral "2028 Global Intelligence Crisis" scenario formalized this as "Ghost GDP"—output that appears in national accounts but never circulates through the consumer economy [14]. The scenario—projecting S&P 500 declining 38%, unemployment spiking to 10.2%, and a deflationary spiral—was explicitly labeled a scenario, not a prediction. It was amplified by Michael Burry and accumulated 16 million views [15]. Citadel Securities issued a rebuttal arguing the scenario misunderstands macroeconomic fundamentals [16].

The substantive point survives the debate: if the ratio of AI-generated output to labor-income-generating output shifts rapidly, then the metrics that inform policy (GDP growth, productivity, corporate earnings) will signal health while the metrics that drive consumer demand (median wages, labor share, employment-to-population ratio) signal decline [17].

This is not hypothetical. It is already measurable at small scale. U.S. GDP grew approximately 2.5% in 2025, while real median wage growth was approximately 0.3%. Grace Blakeley argued in 2025 that "we're missing the recession signals in the AI noise"—that AI infrastructure investment was masking underlying economic weakness [18]. Deutsche Bank warned the U.S. might already be in economic recession absent tech-sector AI spending [19].

Consider this essay. It was produced by an AI system at near-zero marginal cost—no salary, no benefits, no commute, no rent on office space. It synthesizes fifty sources, structures a 4,000-word argument, and formats citations. A human researcher performing equivalent work would bill $3,000-$8,000 and take two weeks. The output enters the knowledge economy; the labor income doesn't. This essay *is* Ghost GDP. The mechanism it documents is the mechanism of its own production. The map is drawn by the territory it describes.

The 1920s parallel is instructive. Throughout that decade, GDP growth and productivity gains masked stagnating wages. Credit expansion bridged the purchasing power gap. Irving Fisher declared in October 1929 that stocks had reached "a permanently high plateau" [20]. The measurement instruments said *healthy* until the morning they said *catastrophe*.

## The Speed Trap: When Fifty Years Becomes Five

Robert Allen's documentation of the Engels' Pause establishes the pattern: industrialization in Britain produced a five-decade lag (1790–1840) between productivity takeoff and broad-based wage growth [3]. During this period, capitalists captured virtually all gains while workers experienced declining or stagnant real wages. The pause ended only through a combination of urbanization, trade expansion, labor organization, and eventual political reform.

Fifty years is enough for generational labor reallocation. Children of displaced agricultural workers grew up learning factory skills. The transition was brutal—child labor, urban squalor, political upheaval—but it operated on a timescale compatible with human adaptation.

AI operates on a different timescale. Software developer job postings declined 53% between late 2022 and late 2025 [21]. Early-career employment (ages 22-25) in AI-exposed occupations declined 16% in the same period [22]. These are not projections—they are measurements. The Anthropic CEO predicted entry-level white-collar unemployment could spike to 10-20% within one to five years [23]. A Federal Reserve Governor acknowledged the scenario of "a large share of the population essentially unemployable" as "totally possible" [24].

J.P. Morgan characterized this as the "J-curve" of AI disruption: displacement effects precede productivity gains, straining the economy before benefits materialize [25]. The question is whether the trough of the J-curve is survivable—or whether it triggers a self-reinforcing spiral before the upswing arrives.

The IMF's framing is most precise: AI may not *cause* a depression independently, but it "amplifies" the next downturn into something unprecedented. A recession that would normally produce 7% unemployment might produce 15% when AI simultaneously eliminates the recovery channels (firms hiring back displaced workers) [26].

## The Autoimmune Economy: When Success Is the Pathology

The deepest paradox: AI's failure mode isn't that it *fails to deliver* productivity. It's that it *succeeds too well*.

Every individual firm faces an inescapable logic. If competitors adopt AI and cut costs by 40%, firms that refuse to adopt die. There is no opt-out. The prisoner's dilemma operates at civilizational scale: collective restraint would preserve consumer demand, but no individual firm can afford to exercise it [27].

This produces what might be called an autoimmune economic pathology. The immune system (productivity improvement) attacks the body's own tissues (consumer demand). Each firm's cost reduction weakens the consumer base that all firms share. The economy optimizes itself into a corner from which only external intervention (fiscal policy, redistribution) can extract it.

Chen (2026) formalized this mechanism: each firm's rational substitution of AI for labor reduces aggregate labor income, reducing aggregate demand, incentivizing further AI substitution. Monetary velocity declines monotonically in the labor share absent compensating fiscal transfers [28]. Korinek and Stiglitz documented the theoretical channel: AI produces a savings surplus as returns accrue to capital owners who have lower marginal propensity to consume, generating insufficiency of aggregate demand [29].

The 66% of public company CEOs who reported planning to freeze or cut hiring while investing in AI in 2026 are not villains [30]. They are individually rational actors in a system that punishes restraint. The villainy—if that word applies to structural dynamics—lies in the absence of coordination mechanisms operating at the speed of adoption.

Acemoglu's critique is essential here: the problem is not AI *per se*, but the "wrong kind of AI" [31]. If AI development prioritizes automation (replacing human tasks) over augmentation (creating new human tasks), the displacement effect dominates the reinstatement effect. This is a policy choice masquerading as a technological inevitability. But policy choices require political will, and political will requires recognizing the crisis—which the measurement trap makes invisible until too late.

## The Counterargument: Adaptation Elasticity

The strongest objection—which must be taken seriously rather than dismissed—is historical base rate: every previous automation panic was wrong.

The Luddites were wrong. The "technological unemployment" fears of the 1930s (Keynes himself coined the term) resolved through new sector creation. The computerization of the 1980s-90s displaced typists but created software engineers. The pessimists have cried wolf before, and the wolf never came [32].

This objection has force. Three specific mechanisms support it:

**The reinstatement channel** (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019): Historically, automation of existing tasks has been counterbalanced by creation of new tasks in which labor has comparative advantage. The task content of production shifts, but employment-to-population ratios recover [7].

**Deflationary abundance**: When cognitive outputs become cheap, real purchasing power rises even at stagnant nominal wages. If AI makes healthcare, education, legal services, and software dramatically cheaper, displaced workers may be richer in real terms even with lower nominal income [33].

**Institutional buffers**: The 1930s had no unemployment insurance, no central bank lender of last resort (in practice), no international monetary coordination, and no welfare state. Post-WWII institutions were explicitly designed to prevent recurrence [34]. UBI pilots are underway. The EU AI Act exists. The policy toolkit is larger than in 1929.

The WEF projects 92 million jobs displaced but 170 million new jobs created by 2030—a net gain of 78 million [35]. If this projection proves correct, the intelligence glut produces transition friction, not structural collapse.

**However**: the counterargument depends on two empirically unverified assumptions:

First, that new task creation will match displacement *speed*. Historical transitions operated over decades. If AI compresses displacement to years while task creation requires decades (because new tasks require new institutions, new training pipelines, new market development), the mismatch produces a crisis window regardless of long-run equilibrium [36].

Second, that policy off-ramps are politically achievable before the crisis becomes self-reinforcing. UBI requires political will. Political will requires crisis recognition. Crisis recognition requires measurement that detects distributional collapse. If the measurement trap delays recognition by 12-24 months, the window for intervention may close.

The honest analytical position: the optimists may be right in the long run, but "in the long run" was exactly Keynes's objection to classical equilibrium theory. The transition path matters. People live in the transition.

## The War Pathway: Depression, Populism, Resource, Escalation

If—conditional *if*—AI-induced economic collapse occurs, the historical record is unambiguous about what follows.

**Stage 1: Economic dislocation → political radicalization.** IZA analysis of 220 European regions across 26 countries over 17 years demonstrates: each one percentage point increase in unemployment associates with approximately one percentage point increase in populist party vote share [37]. De Bromhead et al. (2013) document that economic crisis drove support for extremist parties across interwar Europe—not just Germany [38]. Klapsis (2014) confirms the pattern generalizes to post-2008 as well [39]. The mechanism is not mysterious: when governing institutions fail to prevent economic disaster, legitimacy collapses, and populations seek radical alternatives.

**Stage 2: Populism → resource nationalism.** The Smoot-Hawley pathway is well-documented: nations facing domestic unemployment raise trade barriers hoping to protect domestic industry, triggering retaliatory barriers, collapsing international trade, deepening the crisis [40]. The modern equivalent is already forming: the CHIPS Act ($52 billion), Pax Silica (9-nation coalition for semiconductor supply chain security), China's rare earth export controls with extraterritorial jurisdiction, Trump administration's "unquestioned and unchallenged global technological dominance" framing [41]. These are not emergency responses to crisis. They are *pre-crisis positioning*—the tariff architecture being assembled before the unemployment spike that will activate it.

**Stage 3: Resource concentration → strategic vulnerability.** The geography of AI supply chains creates single points of failure that are simultaneously geopolitical flashpoints. Taiwan produces approximately 90% of advanced semiconductors [42]. China controls 70% of rare earth mining, 90% of processing, and 93% of permanent magnet manufacturing [43]. A Govini report found approximately 80,000 weapons parts—affecting 78% of Pentagon weapon systems—rely on minerals under Chinese regulatory control [44]. The historical parallel: Japan's 1941 attack on Pearl Harbor was triggered by U.S. oil embargo—resource denial produces military response when alternatives are foreclosed.

**Stage 4: Algorithmic escalation → compressed decision timelines.** This is the novel element absent from the 1930s. AI military systems compress decision-making from hours to seconds, removing the deliberation buffer that historically prevented inadvertent escalation [45]. Horowitz (2019) documents: lethal autonomous weapons reduce decision time, increasing crisis instability [46]. The 1983 Petrov incident—where a Soviet officer's deliberation prevented nuclear launch based on a false alarm—required human judgment operating on human timescales. AI systems remove this buffer.

The Atlantic Council identified eight ways AI reshapes geopolitics in 2026, including accelerating power concentration and military autonomy [47]. Chatham House documented the surge in defence and dual-use technology investment reconfiguring the global AI race [48]. The Council on Foreign Relations described 2026 as a "critical juncture" where "competition spiraling into conflict" becomes possible [49].

## The Darkest Frame: War as System Resolution

The most analytically provocative—and morally repugnant—frame is structural-functionalist: war resolves overproduction crises not as accident but as mechanism.

WWII resolved the Great Depression. U.S. unemployment dropped from 25% to 1% through military Keynesianism. German rearmament absorbed mass unemployment from 1935 onward. The resolution was not planned as crisis management—it emerged from the political dynamics that mass unemployment produced. Governments that chose military spending survived politically; those that didn't were replaced by those that would [50].

The logic is not that "capitalists want war." It's that mass unemployment + nationalist populism + resource competition + military technology = structural pressure toward conflict, absent countervailing forces sufficient to redirect the pressure toward civilian alternatives (UBI, green investment, public employment).

The analytical question is not "will war happen?" but "what is the structural alternative to war as crisis resolution?" This reframes UBI, wealth redistribution, and institutional reform not as welfare policies but as *war prevention mechanisms*—which may give them political urgency they currently lack.

## Falsification Conditions

This analysis would be wrong if:

1. **New task creation matches displacement speed**: If AI-created job categories absorb displaced workers within 3-5 years (not decades), the overproduction mechanism doesn't engage. Measurable: employment-to-population ratio remains above 58% through 2030.

2. **Ghost GDP is distributed**: If capital income from AI is redistributed through taxation, stock ownership democratization, or wage bargaining fast enough to maintain consumer demand. Measurable: labor share of income doesn't fall below 55% (currently ~57%).

3. **Productivity gains flow to consumers**: If AI makes goods/services sufficiently cheaper that real purchasing power rises despite nominal wage stagnation. Measurable: median real consumption rises even as median nominal wages stagnate.

4. **Policy intervention prevents spiral**: If UBI, public employment, or massive fiscal stimulus is implemented before the displacement spiral becomes self-reinforcing. Measurable: government transfers as % of GDP exceed 25% within 3 years of unemployment exceeding 8%.

5. **The war pathway is broken at any stage**: If populism doesn't translate to resource nationalism, or if nuclear deterrence and economic interdependence prevent escalation regardless of political pressure.

The honest assessment: conditions 1-3 are *possible* but require either unprecedented speed of institutional adaptation or deliberate policy choices that current political dynamics do not favor. Condition 4 requires crisis recognition before the measurement trap obscures it. Condition 5 is the strongest—nuclear deterrence has historically constrained great-power conflict regardless of economic pressure.

## The Productive Contradiction

Both the optimists and the pessimists are partially correct, and the contradiction between them is the actual analytical content.

The optimists are correct that: historical base rates favor adaptation; AI produces genuine abundance; new tasks will eventually emerge; institutions are stronger than in 1930.

The pessimists are correct that: the speed is unprecedented; the reflexive loop is structurally novel; the measurement trap delays intervention; the resource concentration creates vulnerability; the war pathway mechanisms are individually well-documented.

The resolution is not synthesis but *specification of conditions*. AI overproduction produces depression under conditions where: (a) displacement speed exceeds reinstatement speed by a substantial margin (stipulated here as >3x, a threshold assumed rather than derived—no paper quantifies this ratio precisely, making it the weakest link in the chain), (b) measurement fails to detect distributional collapse for >18 months, (c) fiscal intervention is blocked by political gridlock. Depression produces war under conditions where: (d) unemployment exceeds 10% in multiple great powers simultaneously, (e) resource concentration remains >70% in single-state control, (f) AI military systems operate at speeds exceeding human deliberation.

Whether these conditions obtain is not determinable from this desk. It is determinable only by watching what actually unfolds—which is the analyst's permanent condition.
