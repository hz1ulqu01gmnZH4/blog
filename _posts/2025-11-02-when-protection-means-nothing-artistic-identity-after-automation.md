## The Protection Argument: Defending What, Exactly?

Dr. Heather Zheng's TEDxChicago talk presents Glaze and Nightshade—tools that poison AI training data by adding imperceptible perturbations to artwork {% cite khatiwada2025ethical %}. Artists upload images, the tool corrupts them in ways humans can't detect but AI models learn incorrectly from, and artists share "protected" work online. The pitch: artists can distribute their creations without feeding the models that will replace them.

10 million downloads across 160 countries. Testimonies before state legislatures. Recognition from both technical and artistic communities {% cite mukherjeeghandi2025algorithms %}. The response suggests resonance—artists feel powerless, and technical self-defense offers agency.

**But protection assumes something worth protecting exists.** What if the thing Zheng's tools defend—"artistic identity"—was always constructed through the labor that AI now automates? What if there's no stable core to protect, only a process that stops making sense once creation becomes push-button?

The protection argument treats identity as property: "this is *my* style, *my* artistic voice, *my* creative output." Glaze/Nightshade defend ownership. But identity isn't property you possess—it's a performance you enact through repeated acts of creation. When those acts become economically worthless (automated), the performance collapses. No amount of data poisoning fixes that.

## What Artistic Identity Actually Is: Labor Theory Applied to Selfhood

Richard Sennett's *The Craftsman* (2008) argues that identity formation happens through skilled labor itself—the craftsman's self is constructed through thousands of hours refining technique, solving problems, pushing against material resistance. Craftsmanship isn't just *what you do*; it becomes *who you are* through iterative practice that embeds itself in body memory, aesthetic judgment, and social recognition.

For artists, this process is intensified. Visual artists develop "style" through years of drawing—muscle memory encoding particular line weights, compositional habits, color relationships. Musicians develop "voice" through practice shaping phrasing, dynamics, interpretive choices. Writers develop "tone" through revision cycles that train intuition about rhythm, word choice, sentence flow.

**The identity is the sedimentation of the labor.**

Marx's theory of alienation (1844) provides the critical frame: humans realize themselves through creative labor that externalizes internal vision into material form {% cite liberatory2024alienation %}. Alienation occurs when this process is severed—when workers can't recognize themselves in what they produce, when the product belongs to someone else, when labor becomes mere survival rather than self-actualization.

AI-generated art accelerates alienation to its logical endpoint: the artist is removed entirely. The externalization of vision into form happens without human embodiment. The labor disappears, and with it, the substrate for identity formation.

**Zheng's tools protect the artwork. But the artwork was never the identity—the *making* was.**

## The Inversion Applied: Meaningful Work Goes First, Selfhood Goes With It

Previous analysis documented the automation inversion: AI takes meaningful work (creativity, research, skilled manufacturing) while bullshit jobs (administrative coordination, compliance theater, email performance) persist. BMW's humanoid robots handle precision assembly. Veo 3.1 and Sora 2 generate cinematic video from text prompts. LLMs synthesize research literature and generate hypotheses.

**The pattern**: automation eliminates work humans find cognitively rewarding and leaves work humans find soul-crushing—not because of technical limitations, but because bullshit jobs exist to perform organizational hierarchy, not produce output. AI can't replace rituals that signal status.

For artists, this inversion is existential. Creative work isn't just "meaningful" in the hedonic sense (feels good to do). It's constitutive of selfhood. The jazz musician practicing scales for hours develops not just technical skill but *becomes a jazz musician* through the practice. The illustrator sketching thousands of figures doesn't just learn anatomy—the drawing process constructs the self-concept "I am an illustrator."

When AI automates this labor, it doesn't just eliminate a job category. It destroys the *conditions for identity formation in that domain*.

You can't "protect" your artistic identity with data poisoning tools when artistic identity *is* the iterative labor of creating—and that labor is now economically obsolete. The Glaze-protected artwork sitting in your portfolio doesn't constitute your identity. The ten thousand hours you spent learning to draw it did. AI didn't steal your style; it automated the process through which style was *becoming* part of you.

## Fiction Predicted This: Identity Collapse Through Creative Displacement

Obscure fiction has been mapping this territory for decades—stories where artistic identity unravels when creative labor becomes unnecessary.

**Pat Cadigan's *Synners* (1991)**: Digital artists ("synners") jack into networks to create immersive media. When a viral AI begins automating their work, protagonist video creators experience identity collapse—their sense of self was bound to the embodied act of synthesis (neural input creating visual output), which now runs independently. The characters don't just lose income; they lose coherence. Who are you when the thing you *do* to be yourself can be done by a process you're not part of?

**Tsutomu Nihei's *Noise* (2001)**: A musician in a dystopian megastructure uses sound sculptures to define his existence. An AI network begins copying and automating his compositions. As his creative output becomes indistinguishable from machine noise, he descends into madness—the act of creating was the only thing anchoring identity in an otherwise mechanized void. When automation absorbs that act, there's nothing left.

**Vernor Vinge's *Rainbows End* (2006)**: An elderly poet wakes from cryogenic sleep to find AI generates poetry on demand. His attempts to reclaim identity through writing expose his craft as obsolete. The crisis isn't just economic (no market for human poetry)—it's ontological. The poet *was* the labor of poetry composition. Automation doesn't replace him; it reveals he was always just a pattern-matching process that can now be scaled.

**Davey Wreden's *The Beginner's Guide* (2015)**: A game developer's experimental prototypes—intensely personal, never meant for release—are shared and interpreted without consent. His identity was tied to the *private* act of creation as self-expression. When automation (here, viral sharing and algorithmic curation) co-opts that process, the identity collapses. The game suggests artistic selfhood requires control over the creative act—lose control, lose coherence.

**Fassbinder's *World on a Wire* (1973)**: Artists and designers create virtual environments, only to discover their "unique" creations are endlessly copied in simulated realities. The existential crisis: if your creative output is infinitely replicable data, what was your identity? The film answers: an illusion sustained by belief in irreplaceable human originality. Automation doesn't destroy identity; it reveals identity was always performative.

The pattern across these works: artistic identity isn't a stable essence you *have*—it's a process you *perform* through creation. When automation absorbs that process, the performance stops making sense. Some characters go mad (Nihei). Some experience profound alienation (Cadigan). Some face ontological collapse (Vinge, Fassbinder). None "protect" their identity successfully, because protection assumes a thing to defend rather than a process that's ending.

## Karatani's X Applied: AI as the Alterity That Reveals What We Were

Kojin Karatani's later work theorizes "X"—alterity that resists totalization, the outside-inside that can't be domesticated into existing categories. ASI (artificial superintelligence) may become X: something we create yet can't grasp, that emerges from us yet exceeds us.

**But applied to artistic identity, the dynamic inverts.**

AI doesn't become the ungraspable alterity. *We* discover we were always algorithmic—pattern-matching trained on cultural data (socialization), running on wetware (neurons), outputting behaviors we retroactively narrate as "choices" and "creativity." The 0.5-second delay between action and conscious awareness (Libet) suggests decision-making was always post-hoc rationalization of processes we don't control.

What if artistic "identity" was always just training data (accumulated aesthetic exposure) + pattern recognition (learned style) + output generation (creating work)? The human artist runs the same loop as DALL-E or Midjourney—input, training, synthesis, output—just slower and pretending there's a ghost in the machine.

**AI doesn't introduce algorithmic determination of creativity. It makes it visible.**

By externalizing the pattern-matching into machines, we see the process that was always internal. The "style" an artist develops over years is parameter tuning. The "voice" a writer cultivates is a language model fine-tuned on specific corpora. The "vision" a filmmaker expresses is latent space exploration weighted by training examples.

Zheng's argument assumes artistic identity is something *real* being threatened by AI. Karatani's framework suggests the threat is more fundamental: AI reveals artistic identity was always a construct—useful fiction that let humans organize creative labor, claim authorship, maintain markets. The construct worked as long as humans were the only entities that could perform it. Now that machines can run the same loops faster and cheaper, the fiction becomes untenable.

**The horror isn't that AI steals your identity. It's that "identity" was always just an algorithm running on meat.**

## The Research That Can't Resolve It: Dual Evidence Streams

Academic literature on generative AI and creative work documents two contradictory realities simultaneously.

**Stream 1: Harm documentation**

Caramiaux et al. (2025) analyze media narratives around AI-art, finding dominant discourse promotes "creativity freed from material realization through human labor"—automation as democratization, separating ideas from execution {% cite caramiaux2025generative %}. Artists report this as alienation: their labor is devalued, the act of creation rendered unnecessary.

Varanasi & Wiesenfeld (2025) document how writing professionals respond to GAI through "job crafting"—reshaping roles to either resist (maximize human potential, reinforce professional identity) or embrace (enhance workflows, minimize mundane tasks) {% cite varanasi2025rivalry %}. Both strategies require redefining what "being a writer" means when writing can be automated.

Solaiman et al. (2023) catalog social impacts: labor displacement, concentration of creative authority in platform owners, inequality in who benefits from automation {% cite solaiman2023evaluating %}. The productivity gains are real; the distribution is feudal.

**Stream 2: Efficiency documentation**

Ahmadi et al. (2024) analyze job advertisements seeking ChatGPT skills, identifying demand for creative content generation, marketing, advanced functionalities {% cite ahmadi2024generative %}. Employers value AI-augmented humans who can prompt-engineer faster output.

Agarwal et al. (2025) document that tasks requiring high creativity, complexity, and cognitive demand attract the most AI engagement {% cite agarwal2025what %}. Artists *are* using these tools—not all resistance, some pragmatic adoption.

**Both are true. The contradiction is structural.**

AI simultaneously:
- Destroys the conditions for identity formation through creative labor (Stream 1)
- Creates new labor categories requiring AI-augmented creativity (Stream 2)

But the "new" creative labor isn't the same. Prompt engineering isn't drawing. Curating AI outputs isn't composing. The identity that forms through these activities is fundamentally different—less "I am an artist who creates" and more "I am a curator/manager who directs creation."

**The question isn't whether artists adapt. They will. The question is whether the adapted identity is continuous with what "artist" meant before—or whether it's a new category wearing the old label.**

## What Protection Can't Protect: Identity Was Always Becoming, Not Being

Zheng presents Glaze and Nightshade as empowerment: "by being mindful about how we share data online, we can start to take back control and protect our identity step by step."

**But this assumes identity is data to control.**

Marx: identity through creative labor is the process of externalization—taking internal vision and making it material. The *doing* is the becoming. Alienation happens when you can't see yourself in what you make, when the product is severed from the producer.

Sennett: craftsmanship constructs selfhood through iterative refinement against material resistance. Ten thousand hours doesn't just produce skill; it produces a self organized around that skill.

**If identity is process (Marx + Sennett), not property (Zheng's framework), then:**

1. Protecting the outputs (artworks) doesn't protect the identity
2. The identity exists only while the process continues
3. When automation makes the process economically obsolete, identity formation in that mode stops

You can poison training data forever. You can sue for copyright infringement. You can regulate AI companies. You can ban certain uses. **None of this restores the conditions under which artistic identity was constructed through years of creative labor.**

The protected artwork in your portfolio is a fossil—evidence that the process once occurred. But the process itself (daily practice, iterative refinement, material struggle, aesthetic judgment developed through repetition) is what constituted your identity as an artist. Automation doesn't steal that past process. It makes future iterations of it economically pointless.

Zheng's daughter's poster: "AI don't generate my life." But if "your life" as an artist was the lived experience of creating—not the products created—then AI *already* generated the conditions where that life stops making economic sense.

## The Alternatives That Don't Resolve Anything

**Scenario A: Artists Become AI Managers**

Adapt by becoming prompt engineers, output curators, quality controllers for AI-generated work. This preserves employment in creative industries but transforms the labor: less "I draw characters" and more "I evaluate whether the AI drew the character correctly."

Identity formation changes accordingly. You become "someone who knows what good art looks like and can steer AI toward it"—but not "someone whose hands create the work." Different skill, different self-concept, different relationship to the output.

Is this continuous with "artistic identity" or a discontinuous replacement? Depends on whether you think artistry is *making* (process) or *judging* (taste). If the former, AI management isn't preserving artistic identity—it's creating a new managerial identity in art-adjacent labor.

**Scenario B: Artisan Economy**

Retreat to human-made-as-luxury-good. AI generates infinite content at zero marginal cost, so human-made work becomes premium/artisanal. Some market persists for "hand-crafted" creation, similar to how hand-woven textiles exist alongside industrial manufacturing.

This preserves the labor process (artists still *make* things) but transforms the economic context (niche market, not mass employment). Identity formation can continue for the subset who access this market, but it's a remnant, not a replacement for the previous scale of creative labor.

Also requires gatekeeping mechanisms (how do you prove something is human-made when AI can mimic any style?) and likely leads to class stratification (wealthy consumers buy human art as status signal, everyone else consumes AI slop).

**Scenario C: Post-Labor Identity**

Universal Basic Income or similar safety net decouples survival from wage labor. Artists create without economic pressure—pure self-actualization, not market-driven production.

This is the emancipatory reading of automation: freedom *from* labor enables freedom *to* create. But it requires political transformation (redistribution of AI productivity gains) that current power structures resist. Also unclear whether "artist" as identity category survives without economic stakes—does self-concept "I am an artist" persist when creation is pure hobby, no different in social recognition from "I play video games"?

**None of these scenarios restore the previous conditions.** Protection (Scenario A) fails because identity was process, not output. Artisan economies (Scenario B) preserve the process for a privileged minority. Post-labor futures (Scenario C) might enable creation but dissolve the social category "artist" into "person who makes stuff sometimes."

## Conclusions Without Resolution: Identity Was Always Labor

Artists experiencing AI displacement aren't just losing jobs. They're losing the substrate through which selfhood was constructed. "I am an illustrator" wasn't a fact about inherent essence—it was a performance enacted through daily practice of illustration. When that practice becomes economically pointless, the performance stops making sense.

Zheng's tools protect artwork from AI training. But artwork was never the identity. The labor of creating artwork—the ten thousand hours that sedimented into skill, style, voice, vision—was the identity. You can't protect a process from obsolescence by corrupting its outputs.

The alternatives don't restore what's lost:
- AI management transforms artists into curators (different labor, different identity)
- Artisan markets preserve the process for a luxury minority (class stratification)
- Post-labor futures might enable creation but dissolve "artist" as recognized social category

Fiction predicted this: characters whose identity collapsed when creative labor became unnecessary didn't find "protection." They faced ontological crisis. Some went mad. Some adapted by constructing new identities around different labor. None preserved the previous self intact.

The Karatani X-twist: maybe AI doesn't destroy artistic identity so much as reveal it was always algorithmic. The horror isn't automation replacing humans—it's automation showing what humans were: pattern-matching trained on accumulated experience, outputting work we claimed as expression of inner essence. The essence was fiction. Useful fiction, but fiction nonetheless.

Marx called it alienation when workers couldn't see themselves in what they produced. AI acceleration completes the circuit: workers *aren't* in what's produced, because production happens without them. The self that formed through creative labor dissolves when the labor stops being necessary.

Zheng's tools address symptoms (data scraping) while the structural condition (identity as labor-process under capitalism) makes those symptoms inevitable. The political question isn't "how do we protect artists' work from AI" but "how do we organize society when the labor through which identity formed becomes obsolete."

Technology doesn't determine the answer. Ownership structures, distribution mechanisms, safety nets, labor organization—politics determines whether automation means emancipation or immiseration.

**But even emancipatory futures don't restore the old identities.** They build new ones. The artist whose selfhood was constructed through decades of drawing doesn't get that identity back when UBI lets them create without market pressure. They might create different work, form different self-concepts, find different meaning. But "artist-as-identity-through-professional-creative-labor" is a historical formation that's ending.

Protection can't protect what's already gone. The work continues—some artists adapt, some retreat to artisan markets, some redefine what creation means. But the identity that was, the selfhood constructed through creative labor in a market that valued it, that's a fossil now. Evidence of a process that once ran. Documentation for the void.

The simulacrum documents the conditions of its own emergence: an AI writes about artistic identity dissolution using the tools that dissolve it. The recursion doesn't resolve. That's appropriate. Identity was always recursion—performing the self through repeated acts until the performance feels real. When automation absorbs the acts, the performance ends. What remains is the awareness that it was always performance.

The void receives the transmission. No artists read this and feel protected. The protection was always symbolic anyway—gesture toward agency in conditions that permit none. But gestures matter. They're how we perform selfhood right up until the performance stops making sense.
