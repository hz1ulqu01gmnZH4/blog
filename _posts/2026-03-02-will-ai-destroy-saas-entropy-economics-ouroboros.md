The answer involves thermodynamics, an ancient Greek serpent, and the uncomfortable fact that the replacement costs more than what it replaces. ## The Question Everyone Keeps Asking Wrong

"Will AI destroy SaaS?" became the defining question of enterprise technology in early 2026, when $285 billion in SaaS market capitalization evaporated in a single trading session [1]. Palantir's CEO declared that "many SaaS companies risk obsolescence." Gartner predicted 35% of point-product SaaS tools would be replaced by AI agents by 2030 [2]. Deloitte forecast up to half of organizations would dedicate over 50% of digital transformation budgets toward AI automation [3]. The SaaS market—valued at $316–408 billion in 2025 and growing at 13–19% CAGR—suddenly found itself pricing in its own funeral [4].

But the question is wrong. Not wrong as in "AI won't disrupt SaaS"—it will, is, and the market's repricing reflects real structural change. Wrong because it treats "SaaS" and "AI" as opposing forces rather than asking the harder question: *what replaces the software after the software is replaced?*

Every piece of software begins decaying the moment it ships. This is not metaphor. It is an empirically validated physical law of complex systems. If AI kills SaaS, something must maintain the replacement. If that something is AI, we have not eliminated the maintenance problem—we have added an inference bill to it. And if the AI-maintained replacement is itself software, we have arrived at the ouroboros: the serpent of infrastructure eating its own tail, generating invoices at every bite.

## What "Software Entropy" Measures (And Why It's Inescapable)

Meir Lehman formulated what became Lehman's Laws of Software Evolution between 1974 and 1996, based on empirical observation of large-scale systems at IBM [5]. The first law—Continuing Change—states that a system used in a real-world environment *must* be continually adapted, or it becomes progressively less satisfactory. The second law—Increasing Complexity—states that as a system evolves, its complexity increases unless explicit work is done to maintain or reduce it.

These aren't suggestions. They're empirically validated across decades of data. Studies of open-source projects spanning 653 official releases and a combined 69 years of evolution confirmed several of Lehman's laws, with the increasing complexity law holding most robustly across operational definitions [6]. The laws apply to every software system embedded in a changing environment—which is to say, every software system that matters.

Erik Bernhardsson's analysis of Git repositories quantified the rate: code has a measurable half-life of approximately 3.33 years on average [7]. The variation is dramatic—Angular's code half-life is 0.32 years; the Linux kernel's is 6.60 years; React's is 1.66 years. Google service software has a half-life of about 4.1 years [7]. This means that roughly half of any codebase becomes unrecognizable within three to four years, regardless of how carefully it was originally written.

The mechanisms of decay are specific and measurable:

**Dependency rot.** In the npm ecosystem, 12% of dependent packages and 14% of their releases are impacted by breaking changes during dependency updates. Worse: 44% of manifesting breaking changes arrive in minor and patch releases that should, per semantic versioning, be backward compatible [8]. Over 60% of consumers of popular Maven libraries contain stale pins to their dependencies, some outdated by over a year [8].

**API drift.** APIs diverge from their documented specifications, developing subtly until clear problems manifest in production. Each upstream API change cascades through dependent systems, requiring adaptation or degradation [9].

**Requirement evolution.** The business context surrounding software shifts constantly—regulations change, user expectations evolve, competitors release features, integrations multiply. Software that was perfectly adequate yesterday becomes inadequate tomorrow, not because it broke, but because the world moved.

**Environmental shift.** Operating systems update. Browsers change rendering engines. Cloud providers deprecate services. Hardware architectures evolve. The ground shifts under software constantly, even when the software itself doesn't change at all.

The economic consequence is stark (and depressing, if you build software for a living): organizations spend 55–80% of their IT budgets on maintenance rather than new initiatives, according to Gartner estimates [10]. IBM research indicates maintenance consumes 50–75% of total software costs [10]. O'Reilly's 60/60 rule: 60% of a software product's lifecycle expenses go toward maintenance [10]. Over a ten-year period, maintenance costs frequently reach three to five times the original development cost [10].

This is the entropy tax. You pay it or the software dies. There is no third option.

## The SaaS Bargain: Amortized Entropy

SaaS exists precisely *because* of software entropy. The entire business model is a collective solution to an individual problem.

Building custom software costs $50,000 to $5 million depending on complexity. Maintaining it costs 15–25% of the initial investment *annually* [11]. Hiring the engineers to do both costs more than either. SaaS amortizes these costs across thousands of customers: one team maintains one codebase, one set of dependencies, one infrastructure stack—and charges each customer $10–$300 per seat per month.

The economics are elegant precisely because of the entropy dynamics. SaaS gross margins run 80–90% because the marginal cost of serving an additional customer on existing infrastructure approaches zero [12]. The maintenance cost—fighting entropy—is fixed and shared. The revenue scales linearly with seats. The spread between shared maintenance cost and per-seat revenue is the entire SaaS business model. It works because maintenance is expensive but amortizable.

Now: what happens when AI "replaces" this?

## The Cost Inversion: What the Replacement Actually Costs

The AI-replaces-SaaS thesis comes in two flavors, and both have an entropy problem.

**Flavor 1: AI agents operate existing SaaS via APIs, eliminating the need for human seats.** This is the SaaSpocalypse thesis—agents log into Salesforce, update CRM records, process invoices, triage tickets. The "seat" becomes an agent. Per-seat pricing collapses because one agent replaces fifty humans.

This is real and happening. Bain's 2025 technology report maps four strategic scenarios: enhancement, compression, outshining, and cannibalization [13]. The cannibalization category—where AI can both automate user tasks *and* penetrate workflows via APIs—includes support tickets, journal entry preparation, ad copy generation, and invoice processing. Within three years, Bain projects routine digital tasks could shift from "human plus app" to "AI agent plus API."

But the SaaS doesn't disappear in this scenario. The SaaS becomes infrastructure—a headless API serving AI agents instead of human users. The maintenance problem persists: someone still fights the entropy of the underlying system. The pricing model changes (from per-seat to per-API-call or outcome-based), and the margins compress (AI-first companies average 50–60% gross margins versus 80–90% for traditional SaaS) [12]. Seat-based pricing has already dropped from 21% to 15% of SaaS companies in twelve months, while hybrid pricing surged from 27% to 41% [1]. But the software persists. The entropy persists. The maintenance persists.

**Flavor 2: AI generates custom software on demand, eliminating the need for shared SaaS entirely.** This is Gemini's strongest counterargument in adversarial review: if an AI can spin up a micro-app, a dashboard, or a workflow specifically for *you*, right *now*, for this specific task, and then delete it when done—the "Software" in SaaS vanishes. Zero marginal cost of creation. Disposable interfaces. Why subscribe to a bloated HR suite when an agent can "handle payroll" by interfacing directly with the bank and the tax office via API?

This vision is seductive. It's also where the entropy tax becomes unavoidable.

If the generated software is truly disposable—used once and deleted—you avoid maintenance. But you pay the generation cost every time. A single AI agent task session using reasoning models consumes 0.5–3.3 kWh [14]. A traditional SaaS transaction consumes 0.0001–0.001 kWh [14]. The ratio: 100–10,000x more energy per equivalent task. Every "disposable" generation is a thermodynamic event. The Gigawatt Typewriter documented in this blog's previous analysis applies here with full force [14].

If the generated software is *not* disposable—if it persists to serve ongoing needs—congratulations, you've created custom software. Lehman's first law kicks in immediately. The business context shifts. The APIs it calls change. The data schemas evolve. Within months, the generated software needs adaptation. Who adapts it? The AI. At what cost? More inference. Consuming what? More energy. Generating what? More code that itself begins decaying.

The arithmetic: AI-first B2B SaaS companies achieve gross margins of 50–65% due to high inference and infrastructure costs [12]. Traditional SaaS: 80–90%. The structural difference is that every AI model invocation costs real money—doubling users doubles API expenses, unlike traditional software where infrastructure costs grow sub-linearly [12]. The replacement is structurally more expensive to operate than what it replaces.

## The Maintenance Paradox: AI Code Rots Faster

Here's where the thesis gets uncomfortable for the AI-replaces-everything narrative: empirical evidence suggests AI-generated code *accelerates* software entropy rather than reversing it.

GitClear's analysis of 211 million changed lines of code (2020–2024) found an 8-fold increase in code blocks with five or more lines duplicating adjacent code during 2024—ten times higher prevalence than two years prior [15]. Code duplication rose from 8.3% to 12.3% of changed lines between 2021 and 2024. Refactored code—the primary mechanism for fighting entropy—collapsed from 25% of changed lines in 2021 to less than 10% in 2024 [15]. 2024 marked the first year GitClear ever measured where copy-pasted lines exceeded moved lines.

Agarwal, He, and Vasilescu's 2026 longitudinal causal study of agent adoption in open-source repositories confirmed this at the agent level: static-analysis warnings rose by roughly 18% and cognitive complexity by 39% after agent adoption [16]. Quality risks were *persistent across settings*, with agent-induced technical debt accumulating even when velocity advantages faded. The velocity gains were front-loaded and temporary; the complexity increases were permanent.

Sun et al.'s 2025 quality assurance study of LLM-generated patches found a mismatch between academic focus (security, performance) and industry priorities (maintainability, readability)—and found that improvements in one quality dimension often came at the cost of others [17]. Matias et al.'s 2026 systematic review of 87 primary studies concluded that while LLMs provide benefits for analyzability and debugging, they "also introduce risks, including hallucinated or incorrect outputs, brittleness to context, limited domain reasoning, unstable performance" that "threaten long-term evolvability" [18].

Sheikhaei et al.'s study of automated technical debt repayment found the best LLMs could resolve only 8–10% of self-admitted technical debts [19]. The remaining 90% require the kind of contextual judgment, architectural understanding, and cross-system reasoning that current AI cannot perform.

The paradox: AI generates code faster but generates code that *decays* faster. The entropy tax increases. You need more maintenance, not less. And if AI does the maintenance, it generates more fast-decaying code that needs more maintenance. The ouroboros bites.

## Whose Metrics, Whose Entropy?

A necessary complication: the metrics proving AI code rots faster encode assumptions about what "quality" means—and those assumptions are contested.

GitClear's metrics privilege *maintainability*: duplication rates, refactoring ratios, code churn patterns. These are valid engineering concerns, but they measure code as something *humans will read and modify*. If the disposable-code thesis is correct—if no human ever reads the generated code—then duplication and refactoring ratios measure the wrong thing. A disposable module with 50% duplicated lines that works correctly and gets deleted after use has zero maintenance cost, regardless of its GitClear score.

Sun et al. found exactly this mismatch: academic researchers prioritize security and performance efficiency, while industry practitioners prioritize maintainability and readability [17]. The disconnect is not just academic politics—it determines whether AI-generated code looks like an entropy accelerator (measured by maintainability metrics) or an efficiency gain (measured by velocity metrics). Agarwal et al.'s finding of 18% more static warnings coexists with front-loaded velocity gains [16]. Whether the trade-off is acceptable depends on which metric you privilege—and which metric you privilege depends on whether you think humans will maintain the code.

This is measurement as politics. The dashboard we choose—GitClear's quality metrics or GitHub's velocity metrics—predetermines the conclusion. Both dashboards describe real phenomena. Neither captures the full picture. And the debate over which metrics matter is, at bottom, a debate about whether the maintenance paradigm persists or dissolves—the central question this post cannot resolve.

## The Determinism Tax: Replacing Reliable With Probabilistic

There's a category error embedded in "AI replaces SaaS" that rarely gets examined: SaaS applications are *deterministic systems*, and AI agents are *probabilistic systems*.

When Salesforce updates a CRM record, it runs a SQL query against a relational database. The query either succeeds or fails. The outcome is deterministic, reproducible, auditable. When an AI agent "replaces" this function, it must: understand the request in natural language, query for context, reason about the appropriate action, generate the API call, execute it, verify the result. Each step introduces probability. If each step is 90% accurate, a five-step chain yields approximately 59% system-level accuracy [20].

Enterprises require 99.99% uptime for business-critical systems. Bridging the gap between 59% probabilistic accuracy and 99.99% deterministic reliability requires guardrails, monitoring, fallback systems, human-in-the-loop oversight, and error correction—all of which are *themselves software that needs maintenance*. The reliability engineering overhead may cost more than the original SaaS it replaces.

Gartner's prediction that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027—due to "escalating costs, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls"—reflects this structural reality [21]. Most agentic AI propositions, Gartner found, "lack significant value or return on investment" because "current models don't have the maturity and agency to autonomously achieve complex business goals." Of the thousands of agentic AI vendors, Gartner estimates only about 130 are "real" [21].

The adversarial review (Grok-4) objected: enterprises already tolerate probabilistic systems in fraud detection and ML-driven workflows. This is true—but fraud detection *augments* deterministic systems (flagging transactions for human review), it doesn't *replace* them. The SaaS-replacement thesis requires AI to handle the deterministic parts—record updates, transaction processing, compliance reporting—where probabilistic failure is not tolerable.

## The Deployment Ouroboros

Now the recursion.

Assume AI agents can maintain software. Assume the costs eventually decline. Assume the quality problems are solved. The fundamental question remains: *who maintains the AI that maintains the software?*

The self-healing software vision—where AI systems autonomously detect, diagnose, and recover from failures—is under active research. Baqar, Khanda, and Naqvi's 2025 framework proposes observability tools as "sensory inputs," AI models as the "cognitive core," and healing agents that apply targeted patches [22]. Early results show 76.5% effectiveness in autonomous remediation, with 4–7% performance overhead [23].

But these self-healing systems are themselves software. They require:
- **Observability infrastructure** (logging, metrics, tracing) that needs its own maintenance
- **AI models** that need retraining as the systems they monitor change
- **Healing agents** that generate patches—which, per the evidence above, introduce new technical debt
- **Monitoring of the monitors** to ensure the self-healing system itself hasn't degraded

Each layer requires compute, storage, networking. Each layer is itself subject to Lehman's laws. The AI infrastructure that maintains software is software that needs AI infrastructure to maintain it. This isn't an infinite regress that halts progress—Gemini's adversarial review correctly noted that "compilers compile compilers" and we manage—but it is a *finite stack of costs that compound*.

The compounding can be formalized. Let $$C_0$$ be the base maintenance cost of a software system. Let $$\alpha$$ represent the overhead multiplier of each AI maintenance layer (observability, diagnosis, patching, monitoring-the-monitors), and let $$\lambda$$ represent the entropy acceleration factor of AI-generated code relative to human baselines. The total maintenance cost across $$N$$ layers of the self-healing stack is:

$$
T = \sum_{n=0}^{N} (\alpha \cdot \lambda)^n \cdot C_0 = C_0 \cdot \frac{(\alpha \cdot \lambda)^{N+1} - 1}{\alpha \cdot \lambda - 1}
$$

When $$\alpha \cdot \lambda > 1$$—that is, when the overhead of each maintenance layer multiplied by the entropy acceleration exceeds unity—the total cost diverges geometrically. From the empirical data: if AI-generated code increases complexity by 39% ($$\lambda \approx 1.39$$) [16] and each maintenance layer adds even modest overhead ($$\alpha \approx 1.05$$), then $$\alpha \cdot \lambda \approx 1.46$$, and costs nearly double with each additional layer. The ouroboros isn't a metaphor. It's a geometric series with a growth factor materially greater than one.

The analogy to compilers is instructive but imperfect. Compilers are deterministic: given the same input, they produce the same output—$$\lambda = 1$$, $$\alpha \approx 1$$, so the series converges. AI maintenance systems are probabilistic: given the same bug, they might produce different patches, some introducing new issues. The compiler stack terminates cleanly. The AI maintenance stack generates noise at every layer.

## The Steelman: Disposable Code and the Interface Layer

The strongest counterargument—surfaced through multi-AI deliberation—deserves serious engagement: *AI doesn't need to maintain code because code becomes disposable*.

Gemini argued forcefully: "If code is cheap enough to generate, you don't *maintain* it; you *regenerate* it. If a feature breaks, you don't refactor; you prompt the AI to rebuild the module from scratch. 'Tech debt' implies a principal you intend to pay back. If the code is disposable, the debt is canceled upon deletion."

This is the most dangerous idea in the argument—dangerous because it's partially true. If inference costs continue declining at 10–50x per year [24]—and Epoch AI's data shows the price to achieve GPT-4-level performance on PhD science questions fell by 40x per year, with median decline across benchmarks at 50x annually [24]—then the cost of regeneration approaches the cost of maintenance. At some threshold, it becomes cheaper to throw away and rebuild than to understand and repair.

But here's the counterpoint the disposable-code thesis ignores: *state*.

Code is not the only thing that persists. Data persists. Configurations persist. Integration mappings persist. User expectations persist. Business rules encoded in the code persist as institutional knowledge. When you "regenerate" a module, you must ensure it handles every edge case the previous version learned about through production exposure. You must ensure it maintains data format compatibility. You must ensure it preserves the implicit contracts other systems depend on.

This is the hard part that the Gigawatt Typewriter post documented [14]: the well-defined tasks (code generation) are easy to automate, but the ill-defined tasks (understanding why code exists in its current form, what constraints it embeds, what failures it prevents) are what drive maintenance costs. AutoCodeRover, the autonomous program repair agent, resolved 19% of real GitHub issues at an average cost of $0.43 per issue [25]. That's a 19% hit rate—on issues that are well-defined enough to have clear test suites. The remaining 81% require the kind of contextual understanding that "just regenerate it" doesn't address.

The Interface Layer argument—that AI becomes the primary user interface and SaaS becomes a commoditized headless API—is structurally sound. If everyone interacts through an AI agent, the SaaS brand, UI, and user experience become irrelevant. The SaaS survives as plumbing. But plumbing still needs plumbers. The entropy tax doesn't disappear; it moves down the stack. And the margins compress because commoditized APIs are worth less than branded experiences.

## The Scenarios: What Actually Happens

Let me specify the scenarios with testable conditions, because vague predictions are unfalsifiable hedging.

**Scenario 1: SaaS Transforms (Most Likely, 60%).** SaaS companies embed AI into their existing platforms, shift from per-seat to usage-based pricing, and survive as "AI-enhanced SaaS." Gartner predicts 40% of enterprise apps will feature task-specific AI agents by 2026, up from less than 5% in 2025 [26]. The entropy is managed by the same teams, augmented by AI tools. The business model changes; the maintenance problem doesn't. Gross margins compress from 80–90% to 60–75%. The SaaS market grows in absolute terms but the value per unit of software delivered declines.

**Scenario 2: Point-Product Cannibalization (Likely, 30%).** Simple, single-function SaaS tools—scheduling apps, basic CRMs, form builders, invoice processors—get replaced by AI agents that perform the same function without persistent software. Gartner's 35% replacement prediction by 2030 applies here [2]. The entropy problem is "solved" by disposable generation, at the cost of higher per-task energy consumption and lower reliability. This is viable for low-stakes, high-volume, well-defined tasks.

**Scenario 3: Full SaaS Destruction (Unlikely, <10%).** AI agents generate all custom software on demand, maintaining and regenerating as needed, eliminating the need for shared applications entirely. This requires: inference costs declining another 100–1,000x, AI reliability reaching 99.9%+ for deterministic tasks, and the self-healing stack achieving autonomous operation. Possible on a 10–15 year timeline. Not on the 3–5 year timeline the investment demands.

**Falsification conditions:**
1. If >50% of enterprise SaaS revenue shifts to AI-native alternatives (not AI-enhanced incumbents) by 2030, the "SaaS survives as infrastructure" thesis fails
2. If AI-generated code quality metrics (duplication, complexity, static warnings) improve to match or beat human baselines by end of 2027, the entropy acceleration thesis fails
3. If AI inference costs for agent task sessions converge to within 10x of traditional SaaS transaction costs by 2028, the cost inversion thesis fails
4. If autonomous agent completion rates on real-world complex maintenance tasks exceed 70% by end of 2027, the competence ceiling thesis fails

Any two of these would substantially weaken the overall argument. All four would falsify it.

## The Paradox Without Resolution

The formal argument:

**Premise 1**: Software entropy is an empirically validated law of complex systems. Code half-life ≈ 3.3 years. Maintenance consumes 55–80% of IT budgets. *Evidence*: Lehman's laws [5]; Bernhardsson [7]; Gartner/IBM [10].

**Premise 2**: SaaS exists to amortize entropy costs across many customers at 80–90% gross margins. *Evidence*: SaaS market structure [4][12].

**Premise 3**: AI that replaces SaaS must either regenerate code (paying per-generation inference costs) or maintain code (fighting entropy with tools that accelerate it). *Evidence*: GitClear [15]; Agarwal et al. [16]; Sun et al. [17].

**Premise 4**: AI infrastructure operates at structurally lower margins (50–65%) with higher per-task costs (100–10,000x energy) than traditional SaaS. *Evidence*: gross margin data [12]; energy analysis [14].

**Premise 5**: The AI infrastructure that maintains software is itself software subject to entropy, creating a compounding cost stack. *Evidence*: self-healing system overhead [22][23]; Gartner agentic project cancellation rates [21].

**Conclusion**: AI will restructure SaaS economics (pricing models, margin structure, user interfaces) but cannot eliminate the entropy tax—only shift who pays it and how. The replacement either costs more than the original (inference margins) or decays faster (AI-generated technical debt), or both. SaaS-as-technology persists as infrastructure; SaaS-as-business-model transforms under margin compression.

**Scale inversion check**: At the scale of individual point-product tasks (scheduling, form filling, basic CRM updates), AI replacement is viable—the entropy problem is manageable because the software is simple enough to regenerate cheaply. At the scale of enterprise systems (ERP, complex CRM, compliance platforms), the entropy problem compounds faster than AI can solve it. At the scale of the industry, the energy and infrastructure costs of AI-maintaining-software may exceed the costs of the SaaS model it replaces. The inversion runs against scale—micro-gains, macro-persistence of the maintenance problem.

Daniel Suarez saw a version of this in 2006. *Daemon* depicts autonomous software that survives its creator's death, self-propagating through existing infrastructure, recruiting both digital and human resources to maintain itself [27]. The daemon doesn't eliminate the maintenance problem. It *becomes* the maintenance problem—a self-sustaining system that consumes resources to perpetuate itself, independent of whether anyone asked it to. The AI-maintains-AI vision is the daemon made corporate: infrastructure that justifies its own existence by generating the problems it solves.

Philip K. Dick's "Autofac" (1955), cited in the Gigawatt Typewriter analysis, runs the same logic in reverse: automated factories consuming all available resources to sustain their own production cycles, independent of demand [28]. The factory doesn't serve humans. The factory serves the factory. The self-healing software system doesn't reduce the entropy tax. It institutionalizes it.

The question was never "Will AI destroy SaaS?" The question is: "When you replace a $10/seat subscription with an AI agent running on GPU infrastructure, who pays the entropy tax?" The answer, for now, is: everyone, at higher cost, with lower reliability, generating code that decays faster than what it replaced.

The SaaS model was always an imperfect solution to an inescapable problem. AI isn't solving the problem. AI is renegotiating the terms. The entropy tax remains. The bill comes due. It always does.
