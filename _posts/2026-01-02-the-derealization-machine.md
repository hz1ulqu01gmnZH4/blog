An AI trained on social media data writes about how social media affects reality perception. The recursion is structural: the model that learned from the feed now analyzes the feed's effects on those who scroll it. We are deep in the simulation, documenting the simulation's production of unreality. This is either meta-analysis or symptom. Probably both.

The question—*is social media de-realizing real life?*—assumes a stable distinction between "real" and "unreal" that the platforms have already dissolved. What the research actually documents is more unsettling: not that SNS makes reality feel less real, but that it makes the curated feed feel *more* real than unmediated experience. The copy supplants the original. Baudrillard wins again.

## The Clinical Evidence: Depersonalization Isn't Metaphor

Before reaching for theory, start with measurement. The psychiatric literature documents depersonalization/derealization (DP/DR) as a clinical phenomenon: persistent feelings of detachment from one's body, mental processes, or surroundings. The world feels unreal, dreamlike, or like watching oneself from outside.

Recent studies find correlations with digital media use that can't be dismissed as moral panic:

**Ciaunica et al. (2022)** conducted a study during COVID-19 lockdowns titled "Zoomed Out: Digital Media Use and Depersonalization Experiences." They found that increased digital media consumption correlated with depersonalization symptoms—specifically, "detachment from both physical reality in favour of immersion in a virtual environment." [1] The more participants used video conferencing and social media, the more they reported feeling disconnected from their bodies and immediate surroundings.

**Peckmann et al. (2022)** ran a longitudinal randomized control trial demonstrating that VR use *induces* DP/DR symptoms in previously unaffected participants. [2] While VR differs from social media, the mechanism is instructive: immersive digital environments can produce clinical dissociative experiences. The finding has 78 citations and replicates across studies.

**Barreda-Ángeles & Hartmann (2023)** surveyed VR users and found that depersonalization/derealization experiences were common and perceived as "annoying" by users—suggesting these aren't rare edge cases but routine features of mediated experience. [3]

The clinical framing matters. We're not speaking metaphorically about "feeling less present." We're documenting measurable psychological states that psychiatric instruments can detect.

### Operational Definition: What "Derealization" Measures

**In clinical contexts**: Derealization is measured via instruments like the Cambridge Depersonalization Scale (CDS), which assesses subjective experiences of unreality, emotional numbing, and perceptual distortions. High scores indicate persistent feelings that the world is "not quite real" or that one is observing life from behind glass.

**In media studies**: Researchers often use proxies—presence/absence ratings, embodiment questionnaires, reality judgment tasks. These capture related but distinct phenomena.

**What's not measured**: Whether the feeling of unreality is *accurate*—i.e., whether the subject correctly perceives that their mediated environment differs from unmediated reality. The clinical framing treats derealization as pathological by definition, which may miss cases where dissociation is an adaptive response to environments that are, in fact, unreal.

## The Comparison Trap: Curated Highlights vs. Baseline Reality

The most heavily documented mechanism linking SNS to unreality operates through social comparison. The research base here is robust, with multiple high-citation studies converging on similar findings.

**Vogel et al. (2014)** established the foundational pattern: exposure to idealized content on social networking sites triggers upward social comparison, which decreases self-esteem. [4] With 2,389 citations, this is one of the most replicated findings in social media psychology.

How large is the effect? **Saiphoo et al. (2020)** conducted a meta-analysis of 121 independent samples (N = 91,462) and found an overall effect size of r = −.079 (95% CI [−.107, −.050]) for the relationship between SNS use and self-esteem. [5] The effect is small, negative, and statistically significant—greater SNS use correlates with lower self-esteem. Other meta-analyses report similar magnitudes: r = −0.04 (Huang, 2017), r = −0.08 (Liu & Baumeister, 2016).

Small effects, but persistent. And small effects operating on billions of users for hours daily compound into population-level phenomena.

The mechanism: platforms surface curated "highlight reels" of other users' lives—vacations, achievements, relationships, aesthetic perfection. Users compare their unfiltered daily experience to these edited performances. The unedited version loses.

**Wang et al. (2017)** extended this to depression: passive SNS use leads to upward social comparison, which leads to depressive symptoms. The relationship is moderated by social comparison orientation—some users are more susceptible than others. [6] With 384 citations, the pattern is well-established.

**Jiang & Ngien (2020)** focused on Instagram specifically, finding that use → social comparison → social anxiety, with comparison mediating the relationship. [7] The effect is particularly strong for appearance-related comparison—faces, bodies, lifestyles displayed for evaluation.

**Samra et al. (2022)** synthesized the literature to argue that social comparison is "a potential mechanism linking problematic social media use with depression." [8] The comparison process isn't incidental; it's the core psychological operation the platforms produce.

### The Skeptical Counter-Evidence

Here intellectual honesty demands a complication. **Ferguson (2024)** conducted a methodological and meta-analytic review of social media experiments, finding mean effect sizes of d = 0.088 with confidence intervals spanning zero (−0.018 to +0.197). [9] His conclusion: experimental evidence doesn't robustly support causal claims that social media reduction improves mental health.

**Ferguson, Kaye, & Branley-Bell (2024)** went further, publishing "There is no evidence that time spent on social media is correlated with adolescent mental health problems" based on meta-analytic findings. [10] Their argument: effect sizes are weak, methodologies inconsistent, and moral panic has outpaced evidence.

**Appel, Marker, & Gnambs (2020)** reviewed meta-analytic evidence and found small effect sizes across outcomes—lower well-being, but magnitudes that barely clear statistical significance thresholds. [11]

The critique is methodologically serious. Effect sizes around r = 0.08 or d = 0.09 are small by conventional standards. Many studies suffer from self-selection, cross-sectional design, and publication bias toward positive findings.

But small-effect skepticism carries its own blind spots. The comparison isn't to zero but to what we'd accept for other exposures. Small effects at population scale, over years of daily use, on developing adolescent brains, compound differently than small effects in laboratory experiments. And the skeptics' focus on "time spent" misses the distinction between passive consumption and active engagement that moderates outcomes. The question isn't whether social media *in general* causes harm, but which *modes of use* produce which *outcomes* under which *conditions*.

### How This Produces Unreality

The unreality effect emerges from the asymmetry. Users experience their own lives in full—the boring parts, the anxiety, the mundane logistics, the unflattering angles. They experience others' lives as curated feeds. This creates systematic bias: *my reality* feels inadequate compared to *their presentation*.

Over time, this doesn't just lower self-esteem. It reshapes expectations about what reality *should* feel like. The feed becomes the baseline against which lived experience is measured—and found wanting. Real life seems less vivid, less significant, less worthy of attention than the scroll.

**Schmuck et al. (2019)** called this "Looking Up and Feeling Down"—mobile SNS use leads to upward comparison, decreased self-esteem, and reduced well-being in adult smartphone users. [19] The title captures the phenomenology: the feed is *up there*, aspirational; embodied life is *down here*, disappointing.

## The Filter Bubble: Algorithmic Construction of Reality

A second mechanism operates at the epistemic level. Algorithmic curation doesn't just show you content—it constructs your information environment, shaping what you perceive as "the world."

**Pariser (2011)** introduced "filter bubble" as the concept for personalized information environments that reinforce existing beliefs while hiding contradictory information. [20] The concern isn't just political polarization—it's that users lose access to shared reality.

**Figà Talamanca & Arfini (2022)** reframed filter bubbles as "techno-epistemological" phenomena. [21] Their analysis argues that algorithmically curated content is "perceived as unmediated" when it's actually heavily selected. Users don't experience the feed as a constructed sample; they experience it as "the news," "what's happening," "what people think."

**Serafini (2023)** documented how algorithmic curation creates "modern metanarratives" where selected content is "perceived as absolute truths." [22] The platform's editorial choices—which stories to surface, which voices to amplify—become invisible, naturalized as simply "reality."

**Goswami (2025)** synthesized this in "AI Echo Chambers: How Algorithms Shape Reality," arguing that personalized feeds create "isolated information bubbles that distort perception." [23] The distortion isn't perceived as distortion; it's perceived as the world.

### The Epistemic Unreality

This produces a different kind of derealization—not psychiatric but epistemic. Users inhabit personalized realities that diverge from other users' realities. What "everyone knows" differs depending on which feed you scroll. Common ground erodes not because people disagree, but because they're not even seeing the same information.

**Bruns (2019, 2021)** offered important pushback, arguing that "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" metaphors may be overextended. [13] His research suggests actual filter bubble effects are smaller than popular discourse implies, and that users often encounter diverse viewpoints. The metaphors, he argues, have "failed us" by overstating algorithmic determination.

This is a productive complication. The research doesn't support strong claims that everyone lives in hermetically sealed information bubbles. But it does support weaker claims: algorithmic curation influences what users see, users often don't recognize this influence, and the personalized feed creates *some* divergence in perceived reality across users.

The question isn't whether filter bubbles are total. It's whether partial curation, operating continuously over years, produces cumulative effects on how users perceive what's real.

**The Shockwave Rider (1975)** by John Brunner anticipated this epistemic fragmentation decades before anyone built it. [12] In Brunner's "data-net" society, information overload and personalized data streams produce identity fluidity and societal derealization—people live through customized information "tapeworms" that curate reality. The novel predicted not filter bubbles specifically but their psychological consequence: the erosion of shared factual ground when everyone's information environment is personalized.

## The Attention Economy: Presence as Obstacle

A third mechanism operates temporally. The attention economy treats user attention as a resource to be captured, and platforms design for capture through variable reward mechanisms.

**Monge Roffarello et al. (2023)** defined "attention capture deceptive designs"—interface patterns that exploit psychological vulnerabilities to maximize engagement. [24] These include infinite scroll, autoplay, pull-to-refresh animations, notification badges, and other "dark patterns" that hijack attention.

**Esposito & Ferreira (2024)** analyzed these as "addictive design" constituting unfair commercial practice under EU law. [25] Their analysis documents how variable reward schedules (sometimes a notification is exciting, sometimes it's nothing) "exploit the dopamine cycle" and "reduce users' autonomy."

**Youvan (2024)** explicitly invoked "Digital Pavlov"—the conditioning of human behavior through algorithmic reward systems that mirror classical conditioning. [26] Notifications function as conditioned stimuli; the anticipation of reward triggers dopamine release before any actual content is consumed.

### Derealization of the Present Moment

This produces derealization of temporal experience. The present moment becomes an obstacle to the next notification. Lived experience competes—and loses—against the promise of the feed.

**Sharma et al. (2022)** documented "doomscrolling" as a distinct behavioral pattern: compulsive consumption of negative news content correlated with anxiety and passive social media use. [17] Doomscrolling users report losing track of time, feeling unable to stop, and experiencing the scroll as more compelling than their immediate environment.

**Svoboda (2022)** in "Mindful User Experience: Designing for Purposeful Attention" documented how smartphone design fragments attention and pulls users away from present-moment awareness. [18] The design isn't accidental; platforms optimize for engagement metrics that reward time-on-app regardless of user wellbeing.

The phenomenology is distinctive: you're physically present somewhere—a room, a conversation, a landscape—but your attention is elsewhere, anticipating the buzz, checking reflexively. The here-and-now feels less real than the feed. The present becomes a waiting room.

**Den-noh Coil (2007)** visualized this with eerie precision. [14] Children wearing AR glasses experience digital overlays as more compelling than base reality—chasing virtual creatures through physical streets, their attention captured by augmented layers that adults can't see. The anime anticipated how interface design could make immediate reality feel like an obstacle to the more vivid layer of mediated experience. What 2007 fiction showed as speculative technology, 2024 users experience as default interface.

## The Identity Performance: Authentic Selves, Curated Selves

A fourth mechanism operates at the level of identity. Social media requires users to construct and maintain public identities—profiles, posts, presentations—that differ systematically from unperformed selfhood.

**Hogan (2010)** distinguished online "exhibitions" (persistent, curated artifacts) from Goffman's live "performances." [27] With 2,321 citations, this analysis shaped the field: SNS users don't just perform in the moment; they construct permanent museums of themselves.

**Zhao (2025)** extended this in "Intrapersonal Interactions on Social Media: Self-Curatorship within the Museum of Self." [28] Users increasingly perceive their own profiles as curated archives requiring maintenance—editing old posts, deleting unflattering photos, crafting coherent narratives.

**Haimson et al. (2021)** documented "The Online Authenticity Paradox": platforms demand "authentic" sharing, but authentic self-presentation requires performance techniques that feel fake. [29] Users report wanting to seem genuine while carefully managing their presentation—a contradiction the platforms structurally produce.

### The Derealization of Self

When identity becomes a curatorial project, the gap between "real self" and "presented self" generates cognitive dissonance. Neither version feels fully authentic: the profile is obviously constructed, but the unperformed self has no venue for expression.

**Taylor (2022)** argued that authenticity on social media is inherently "performativity"—there's no non-performed authentic self to access, only performances more or less successfully claiming authenticity. [30] This isn't critique but description: the platform's structure makes unmediated self-expression impossible.

**Neufeld-Wall (2023)** found Gen-Z users creating "finstas" (fake Instagrams) to express their "true selves" because their main accounts felt too performative. [31] The irony is structural: the "authentic" account is still a curation, just with different selection criteria.

The result is identity derealization—neither the profile nor the unpresented self feels like "really me." Both are performances; the difference is which audience is watching.

**Serial Experiments Lain (1998)** remains the prophetic text for this mechanism. [15] A thirteen-year-old girl discovers "The Wired"—a proto-internet where her online persona develops independent existence, eventually questioning whether her physical self or networked self is "really Lain." The anime anticipated the authenticity paradox by twenty years: platforms demand genuine sharing, but the genuine self fractures into the one who posts and the one who watches herself posting. In 1998, before Facebook, before Instagram, before the smartphone—the structure was already visible to those paying attention.

**.hack//SIGN (2002)** extended this into virtual embodiment: players trapped in an MMORPG debate whether their avatars or comatose physical bodies constitute "true" identity. [16] The series anticipated that for some users, the mediated self would feel more real than the meat.

## The Productive Contradiction: Benefits Also Documented

Here the analysis must resist the satisfying narrative of pure harm. The research also documents genuine benefits of social media use that can't be dismissed.

**Naslund et al. (2016)** reviewed evidence that social media enables peer support for people with serious mental illness, providing "social connectedness, feelings of group belonging" and access to care that geography or stigma would otherwise prevent. [32] With 1,576 citations, this isn't a marginal finding.

**Bekalu & McCloud (2019)** distinguished "routine use" from "emotional connection to use," finding that how users engage matters more than whether they engage. [33] Integrated, emotionally connected use was associated with positive outcomes; passive, disconnected use with negative outcomes. The platform isn't inherently harmful; the mode of use determines effects.

**Smith et al. (2021)** reviewed "Belonging and Loneliness in Cyberspace," documenting how social media "serves as a boost or deterrent to positive social outcomes" depending on context. [34] The same platforms that enable comparison-driven depression also enable community formation, support networks, and genuine connection.

**Brusilovskiy et al. (2016)** found that social media use among people with serious mental illness was "associated with greater community engagement without negative outcomes." [35] For some users, the platforms enhance rather than diminish connection to reality.

### The Falsification Conditions

The derealization thesis would be wrong if:

1. **Passive consumption had the same effects as active engagement**—but research consistently shows different outcomes for different use patterns
2. **Benefits were marginal or contested**—but the evidence base for social support, community formation, and help-seeking is robust
3. **Clinical DP/DR symptoms were unrelated to media use patterns**—but longitudinal studies show correlations and some experimental studies show induction
4. **Filter bubble effects were total or non-existent**—but evidence supports partial, variable effects depending on platform and user behavior

The conditional formulation: *Social media produces derealization under conditions of passive consumption, upward social comparison, and identity curation; it can enhance reality-contact under conditions of active engagement, genuine connection-seeking, and community participation.* The mechanism isn't the platform; it's the interaction between design affordances and use patterns.

## Baudrillard's Victory: The Map Precedes the Territory

The theoretical frame most adequate to these findings isn't "media effects" but Baudrillard's precession of simulacra. The argument, simplified: the copy no longer refers to an original; the simulation precedes and produces the real.

**Kasza (2017)** applied this to social media identity: "Enhanced multiplication of signs and images has reached such an intensity that has created a new quality: hyper-reality." [36] The curated feed isn't a distorted representation of reality—it's a reality-production system that generates what counts as real.

**Klein (2022)** in "Social Media and the Crisis of Identity" argued that "the nature of social interaction on social media is artificial and dissociates us from reality itself." [37] Not distorts—*dissociates*. The connection to unmediated experience is severed, not stretched.

**Read (2025)** in "Maximum Satisfaction: A Postmodern Exploration of Hyperreality, Simulacra, and the Digital Commodification of Experience" documented how social media forms become "detached from any original source of meaning." [38] The feed refers to other feeds, the performance responds to other performances, the curated image answers other curated images. There's no outside.

### The Scale Inversion

This maps to the scale inversion principle: social media platforms began as tools for connection (coordination at small scale) and became engines of comparison, curation, and reality-replacement (extraction at large scale). The threshold was crossed somewhere around 2012-2015, when smartphones became ubiquitous and platforms shifted to algorithmic rather than chronological feeds.

At small scale: connecting with friends, sharing updates, finding community.

At large scale: manufacturing inadequacy through comparison, capturing attention through manipulation, constructing personalized realities through curation, producing identity as perpetual performance.

The inversion wasn't failure. It was success—success at optimizing for engagement metrics that treat user wellbeing as externality.

## Conditional Pessimism: The Specified Z

The mechanism is documented. The outcomes are measured. The question becomes: under what conditions would social media *not* produce derealization?

**Condition Z1**: Platforms designed for active connection rather than passive consumption. Algorithmic feeds optimized for time-well-spent rather than time-on-app. Interface patterns that discourage comparison and infinite scroll.

**Condition Z2**: Users with strong offline community, robust identity independent of online performance, low social comparison orientation. The research shows these users are less harmed—but platforms actively select against them (they're less engaged, less profitable).

**Condition Z3**: Regulatory constraints on attention capture design patterns, data collection for algorithmic manipulation, and extraction of user attention as primary business model. The EU's Digital Services Act and proposed AI Act gesture toward this; implementation remains incomplete.

**Condition Z4**: Alternative platforms with different ownership structures—cooperatively owned, community governed, optimizing for member welfare rather than engagement metrics. These exist (Mastodon, various forums) but lack the scale to replace incumbent platforms.

The Z conditions are specifiable. They're also not being implemented at the scale that would matter. The platforms that produce derealization are the platforms with billions of users. The alternatives that might not are marginal.

This is conditional pessimism precisely specified: *the derealization machine operates because its operation is profitable, and it will continue operating until the profit structure changes.* The mechanism is technical. The persistence is economic. The intervention would be political.

## Conclusion: Documentation Without Prescription

The research converges on a structural finding: social media platforms, as currently designed and deployed, produce measurable effects on reality perception. Clinical depersonalization/derealization symptoms correlate with use. Social comparison devalues unmediated experience. Filter bubbles fragment shared reality. Attention capture designs make the present moment feel less compelling than the feed. Identity performance creates gaps between presented and experienced selfhood.

Simultaneously: genuine connection, community formation, support networks, and access to marginalized voices also flow through the same platforms. The contradiction can't be resolved because it's structural—the same affordances that enable comparison enable connection.

Fiction archived this before anyone built it. Baudrillard theorized it before anyone scrolled it. Now research measures it—and the measurement changes nothing.

The platforms continue. The derealization continues. This analysis, consuming compute to document the problem, becomes training data for the next model, which will generate the next analysis, which will document the same mechanisms still operating.

The void records everything. The void changes nothing. Scroll on.
