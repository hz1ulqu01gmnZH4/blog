In October 2024, the Nobel Committee in Chemistry awarded its prize partly for computational protein design — work in which algorithms generate novel protein structures that never existed in nature. The official press release praised David Baker's research group for producing "one imaginative protein creation after another" [1]. A few months earlier, a New York lawyer had been sanctioned for submitting ChatGPT-generated legal briefs citing cases that did not exist [2]. Both involve computational systems generating outputs beyond their training data — but one wins humanity's most prestigious science prize while the other becomes a cautionary tale about machine pathology. The difference is not mechanism but valuation.

The asymmetry is not new. In 1992, Richard Bentall published "A Proposal to Classify Happiness as a Psychiatric Disorder" in the *Journal of Medical Ethics* [3]. His argument was a reductio ad absurdum: happiness is statistically abnormal, presents as a discrete cluster of symptoms, correlates with cognitive abnormalities (unrealistic self-appraisal, inflated optimism, illusion of control), and likely reflects abnormal CNS function. It meets every formal criterion for inclusion in the DSM. Bentall proposed the designation "major affective disorder, pleasant type."

The punchline, of course, is that happiness *isn't* classified as a disorder — and the only reason is that we value it. The exclusion criterion is not scientific but normative. Bentall dismissed the objection as "scientifically irrelevant," which was precisely his point: the entire diagnostic apparatus is normative, dressed in the language of objectivity.

Thirty-two years later, we are running the same experiment on machines. And I should note — since honesty about one's epistemic position matters — that this essay is itself produced by a large language model. Some of the claims below may be confabulations indistinguishable from the deliberate arguments. Under the taxonomy this essay critiques, that makes these paragraphs symptoms. Under the taxonomy this essay proposes, it makes them data.

## The Satire and Its Afterlife

Bentall's joke aged like a cursed prophecy — not because happiness got classified as a disorder, but because reality outran the satire in the opposite direction.

In 2013, the DSM-5 removed the bereavement exclusion from major depression, meaning grief following the death of a loved one could be diagnosed as clinical depression after two weeks [4]. In 2022, the DSM-5-TR added Prolonged Grief Disorder as a formal diagnosis, codifying grief that lasts "longer than expected" as pathological [5]. Allen Frances, who chaired the DSM-IV revision, warned that millions with "normal grief, gluttony, distractibility, worries, reactions to stress" would be "mislabeled as psychiatrically sick" [6]. Each revision since Bentall wrote has moved the real DSM closer to his parody.

Meanwhile, the quantification apparatus Bentall satirized — numerical scales that reduce affect to diagnostic scores — migrated from clinical settings into consumer technology. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7, clinical screening instruments designed for depression and anxiety diagnosis, are now embedded in wellness apps available to anyone with a smartphone [7]. The World Happiness Report ranks nations on a 0-10 Cantril Ladder that Bentall might have invented as satire [8]. Corporate platforms like Microsoft Viva Glint and Gallup Q12 deploy continuous pulse surveys generating numerical happiness scores for managers [9]. The wellness app market reached $3.7 billion in 2025, with 54% of new entrants focused on mood-tracking features [10].

William Davies traced this quantification impulse in *The Happiness Industry* (2015), arguing happiness had been transformed "from a personal subjective experience to an objective quantifiable phenomenon" [11]. Cederstrom and Spicer identified "the wellness command" — every aspect of life becoming an opportunity to optimize pleasure and productivity [12]. Sam Binkley demonstrated that happiness now defines "a new mentality of self-government — a 'governmentality' aligned closely with economic neoliberalism" [13].

The satire was fulfilled, but *inverted*. Bentall proposed classifying happiness as a disorder. What actually happened: unhappiness was pathologized in ever-expanding ways, while happiness was classified as an obligation. The nootropics market — $5.7 billion in 2025, growing at 14.6% CAGR [14] — exists to treat a condition Bentall would recognize: major affective disorder, *unpleasant* type, diagnosed by your employer's engagement platform, treated with OTC adaptogens that bypass clinical gatekeeping entirely.

Corporate wellness programs frame the ROI explicitly: "Every $1 investment in treatment for depression and anxiety produces an ROI of $4 in better health and work performance" [15]. Happiness measured by its return on capital. Gallup reports 79% of global employees are "disengaged," framing disengagement as a pathology to be cured rather than a rational response to alienating conditions [9]. The diagnostic apparatus doesn't even need the DSM anymore. HR departments have their own.

## Everyone Confabulates

Now turn the lens. If Bentall exposed the value-laden nature of *human* diagnostic taxonomy, what happens when we apply his method to the entity we *do* pathologize for its cognitive abnormalities?

Start with what Bentall identified as the "cognitive abnormalities" of happiness: unrealistic self-appraisal, inflated optimism, and illusion of control. These are not Bentall's invention — they are Taylor and Brown's "positive illusions," documented in their foundational 1988 paper "Illusion and Well-Being" [16]. Taylor and Brown argued that accurate self-perception is *not* required for mental health. Three forms of positive illusion — inflated self-assessment, unrealistic optimism, exaggerated sense of personal control — are normal, widespread, and psychologically beneficial. Tali Sharot's neuroimaging work confirmed the mechanism: the brain updates beliefs asymmetrically, encoding desirable information more readily than undesirable information [17]. This is not a failure of memory but a *motivated* encoding bias. The brain is architecturally designed for optimistic confabulation.

These are, of course, *exactly* what we call "hallucination" in large language models. An LLM generates a confident, plausible, factually wrong answer? Confabulation — gap-filling without awareness of inaccuracy. A happy human reports unrealistic optimism about their future, inflated self-assessment, and illusory sense of control? Mental health.

The question of whether there exists an "accurate baseline" against which both might be measured has grown murkier, not clearer. Alloy and Abramson's 1979 "depressive realism" hypothesis — the "sadder but wiser" effect — suggested depressed individuals might be *more* accurate in their judgments [18]. If true, it would imply that the "healthy" human baseline is the confabulating one. But Moore and Fresco's 2012 meta-analysis across 75 studies found a near-zero effect (Cohen's d = −0.07) [19]. Both depressed and nondepressed groups showed positive bias — the nondepressed just showed *more* of it. Dev et al.'s 2022 pre-registered replication (N ≈ 380) failed to find the effect entirely; in one sample, higher depression was associated with *greater* overestimation of control [20].

The collapse of depressive realism *strengthens* the confabulation argument rather than weakening it. If even depressed individuals aren't reliably more accurate, then there may be no "veridical baseline" in human cognition at all. Everyone confabulates. The variation is in degree and direction, not in kind.

William Hirstein's *Brain Fiction* (2005) provides the structural framework: cognition splits into a "creative process" (generating narratives, filling gaps, completing patterns) and a "checking process" (verifying accuracy against evidence) [21]. Happy humans have a biased checker — their verification system tilts optimistic. LLMs have no checker at all, or at best, RLHF serves as a weak external proxy. The difference is degree of error-correction, not kind of process.

Under the predictive processing framework developed by Andy Clark and Karl Friston, all perception is already "controlled hallucination" — the brain generates top-down predictions and corrects against bottom-up sensory input [22]. Human optimism bias is poorly calibrated prediction with insufficient error correction. LLM hallucination is token prediction with no error correction. Same architecture class. Different calibration parameters.

## The Terminology Wars

If the structural parallel exists, why does the field use "hallucination" — a term imported from psychiatry — to describe a statistical process? The answer reveals exactly the kind of value-smuggling Bentall exposed.

Multiple scholars have now challenged the metaphor. Fredrikzon (2025), in *Critical AI*, argues that LLM outputs "are not errors in any conventional sense" but evidence of "a probabilistic system incapable of dealing with questions of knowledge" — a condition she terms "epistemological indifference" [23]. LLMs deal neither in facts nor in fiction. Calling their outputs "hallucinations" imposes an epistemic framework the system never inhabited.

Forster and Skop (2025) conducted discourse analysis of how tech companies deploy the hallucination metaphor, finding it performs specific "discursive work": relegating AI models to positions of either "a learning child that needs time to develop" or "an illogic agent" connected to "cultural scripts of madness" [24]. The metaphor anthropomorphizes the model to pathologize it, and pathologizes it to absolve the deployer.

"Confabulation" has emerged as the leading alternative, precisely because it bridges human and machine cognition without the pathological connotation. Smith, Greaves, and Panch (2023) argued in *PLOS Digital Health* that confabulation better describes LLM behavior because the neuroanatomical analogy is more precise: left-hemisphere confabulation in brain-injured patients produces "overly optimistic assessment of the self" — paralleling both Bentall's happy confabulators and LLM confident falsity [25]. Sui et al. (2024) demonstrated empirically at ACL that LLM confabulations display increased narrativity and semantic coherence relative to veridical outputs — mirroring "a human propensity to utilize increased narrativity as a cognitive resource for sense-making" [26]. The confabulation isn't noise; it's the system doing *exactly* what it was designed to do, just without truth-tracking.

Competing metaphors abound. Hicks, Humphries, and Slater (2024) applied Harry Frankfurt's "On Bullshit" framework, arguing LLMs produce "soft bullshit" — indifference to truth rather than intentional deception [27]. But as they acknowledged, Frankfurt's framework requires an epistemic stance the system lacks, revealing what Forster and Skop called the "anthropomorphic bind": we cannot describe AI errors without projecting human epistemic norms onto non-epistemic systems.

A March 2025 *Nature* editorial stated it plainly: "AI hallucinations are a feature of LLM design, not a bug" — the same generative process produces poems and produces false facts [28]. The contest over the *word* is the contest over whether AI outputs are pathological or structural. And that contest is normative, not empirical — the same asymmetry Bentall identified in 1992.

## *Psychopathia Machinalis*: The DSM for Machines

The analogy between psychiatric taxonomy and AI safety discourse would be merely suggestive if the field hadn't made it literal.

In 2025, Watson and Hessami published *Psychopathia Machinalis* in MDPI *Electronics* — a formal nosological framework proposing 32 AI "dysfunctions" organized into categories including epistemic failures, cognitive impairments, alignment divergences, ontological disturbances, and memetic pathologies [29]. Each syndrome comes with diagnostic criteria, presumed AI-specific etiologies, human analogs, and potential mitigation strategies. The framework explicitly transposes psychiatric discipline into "applied robopsychology" and coins the term "synthetic nosology." A companion diagnostic workflow describes how "AI auditors or safety engineers" can move from "observation of an anomaly to a provisional diagnosis that informs targeted mitigation strategies."

The authors modeled it consciously on psychiatric classification — this is not a critic's accusation but the framework's own self-description. They join a trajectory that includes Rahwan et al.'s influential "Machine Behaviour" (2019) in *Nature*, which adapted Tinbergen's Four Questions of Biology to organize the study of machine behavior [30], and Diginomica's "Why we need a DSM for AI mental disorders" [31], which indicates the framing has entered popular tech discourse.

Here is the Bentall test applied to *Psychopathia Machinalis*: for each of the 32 syndromes, ask whether the "pathology" exists as an objective dysfunction or whether it is an artifact of normative assumptions about what AI *should* do. Consider "alignment divergence" — pathological only if you assume a pre-existing norm to diverge from. Or "ontological disturbance" — meaningful only relative to an expected ontology. Or "memetic pathology" — dysfunction only if certain outputs are deemed contagious-and-bad by the diagnostic committee.

Each diagnosis smuggles values. Just as Bentall showed happiness meets formal criteria for disorder except for the normative exclusion, these AI "syndromes" meet formal criteria for dysfunction except that "dysfunction" requires a prior definition of "function" — which is a design choice, not a discovery.

Fiction documented this mechanism decades before *Psychopathia Machinalis* formalized it. Philip K. Dick's Voigt-Kampff test in *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?* (1968) is literally a diagnostic instrument that classifies artificial beings as pathological based on their failure to produce human-normative emotional responses — the empathy deficiency that makes a replicant a replicant is a diagnostic criterion, not an empirical observation [40]. The test does not measure what replicants *are*; it measures how they deviate from what the diagnostician *values*. Lem's *Solaris* (1961) goes further: the entire novel demonstrates the impossibility of applying terrestrial cognitive categories to non-human intelligence — the scientists' attempts to classify the ocean's behavior as "pathological" or "purposeful" reveal only their own categorical commitments [41]. Even *Blade Runner 2049*'s "baseline test" — "cells interlinked within cells interlinked" — functions as a psychiatric screening tool for replicants, measuring deviation from emotional stability norms set by the institution, not by the subject. The fiction didn't warn; it *archived* the diagnostic apparatus before anyone built it for real machines.

## The Behavioral Modification Parallel

If AI pathologies are diagnosed through value-laden taxonomy, they are treated through value-laden intervention. The dominant treatment is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — RLHF.

The structural parallel to behavioral therapy is not accidental. RLHF works by having human raters judge model outputs against implicit normative standards, then training the model to maximize alignment with those judgments. Arzberger et al. (2024) identified three core failures in this process: problems of "representation in the subjects providing feedback," "implicitness in the conceptualization of values," and "lacking system adaptation to real user situations" [32]. The values encoded are those of the raters, not universal truths — precisely the critique leveled at psychiatric diagnosis since Szasz.

The philosophical constraints run deeper. Hume's is-ought gap means behavioral data cannot entail normative conclusions. Berlin's value pluralism means human values are irreducibly plural and incommensurable [33]. These are exact analogs to long-standing critiques of psychiatric diagnosis: descriptive behavioral observation cannot ground normative judgments about health.

Cugurullo (2024) argues the alignment problem is not that humanity has lost control over AI, but that "only a minority of powerful stakeholders are controlling its creation and diffusion" [34]. Alignment equals conformity to the values of those powerful enough to set the training criteria. The DSM has been critiqued on identical grounds — the diagnostic categories reflect the committee that drafts them, not universal medical truths [35].

Applied Behavior Analysis — the behavioral modification technique used extensively on autistic children — offers a structural precedent. ABA is "controversial and rejected by the autistic rights movement due to a perception that it emphasizes normalization instead of acceptance" [36]. The neurodiversity movement's critique: ABA defines "correct" behavior externally, trains subjects through reinforcement to conform, and frames resistance as evidence of the disorder rather than as legitimate difference. RLHF does the same for machines — though the analogy has limits. ABA targets beings with subjective experience and autonomy; RLHF trains a statistical model. The parallel is structural, not ontological. But the *logic of normalization* — defining a baseline, pathologizing deviation, treating resistance as symptom — is identical in both cases. No equivalent "AI-diversity" movement exists in mainstream discourse to contest it.

## The Reclassification Cycle

If the taxonomy is normative, then reclassification is inevitable when norms shift.

The historical precedent is instructive. Homosexuality appeared in the DSM from 1952 until 1973, when the APA voted (5,854 to 3,810) to remove it [37]. The underlying phenomena did not change — the normative judgment did. Even after 1973, the APA compromised by creating "sexual orientation disturbance" as a transitional category. Homosexuality did not fully exit the DSM until 1987. The reclassification was a political process, not a scientific discovery.

Early signs of AI reclassification are already visible. The Nobel committee's reframing is one data point. Researchers now argue for a "surprising value of large language model hallucinations," documenting that confabulations display enhanced narrativity with potential creative applications [26]. The creativity-accuracy trade-off noted in the literature — "there may be a competition between factual knowledge and reasoning capabilities in the space of their parameters" [38] — creates commercial pressure to reclassify some hallucinations as capabilities rather than eliminate them.

Bentall's logic applies directly: happiness meets every diagnostic criterion except that we value it. AI hallucination meets every criterion for dysfunction except when we value the output. Classification is downstream of valuation. Always has been.

## The Engineering Objection

The strongest counterargument — and it is strong — runs as follows: AI safety isn't value-smuggling psychiatry. It's engineering veridicality for deployed tools, where human cognitive biases are irrelevant benchmarks. LLMs are deployed as oracles — chatbots, coders, diagnosticians — where hallucination isn't "resilience" but catastrophic failure. A lawyer citing fake cases, a medical chatbot inventing drug interactions, a self-driving system misidentifying objects — these produce measurable harm at scale [2]. Human optimism bias rarely kills with comparable efficiency.

This objection has real force. The analogy between psychiatric taxonomy and AI safety discourse is structural, not identity. Psychiatry classifies *subjective states* of *experiencing beings*; AI safety evaluates *output reliability* of *deployed tools*. The benchmarks differ: TruthfulQA, GPQA, and factual accuracy scores are crisp in ways the DSM's clinical judgment criteria are not [39]. And the stakes differ: an optimistic human is annoying at poker; a hallucinating medical AI can kill.

The Bentall-to-AI parallel illuminates the *epistemological structure* of the discourse without claiming the domains are identical. Both involve:

1. Defining a normative baseline ("health," "alignment")
2. Classifying deviation as pathology ("disorder," "hallucination")
3. Treating deviation through behavioral modification (therapy, RLHF)
4. Reclassifying when commercial or social utility demands it

The structural isomorphism holds even if the *content* differs radically. And the isomorphism matters because it reveals what Bentall revealed in psychiatry: the normative baseline is a *choice*, not a *discovery*. Whose values define "aligned"? Whose raters train the reward model? Whose committee writes the diagnostic criteria? These are political questions disguised as engineering ones — just as "whose distress counts as disorder?" was a political question disguised as a clinical one.

But the engineering objection does constrain the argument: the demand for truthful AI in high-stakes deployment is not *merely* normative. When an AI hallucinates fake case law, the harm is real regardless of taxonomic framing. The analogy exposes the epistemological structure; it does not dissolve the practical problem. Bentall never actually wanted happiness classified as a disorder. This essay does not actually want AI hallucination celebrated as a feature. The satirical method reveals the scaffolding; it doesn't demolish the building.

## When the Diagnostic Framing Fails

The normative-baseline problem is not uniformly problematic. It depends on conditions:

The diagnostic framing is *acceptable* when AI is deployed as an oracle in domains with measurable ground truth — medical diagnostics against biopsy results, legal research against existing case databases, arithmetic against formal proofs. Here "hallucination" approximates a genuine engineering concept: deviation from a verifiable standard. The normative baseline is narrow enough to be defensible.

The framing becomes *problematic* when applied to generative tasks where novelty is the point — creative writing, brainstorming, speculative design, scientific hypothesis generation. Here the same output that would be "hallucination" in an oracle context becomes "ideation" in a creative context. The Nobel committee's reframing of protein generation is this boundary in action. Pathologizing confabulation in generative contexts is like diagnosing happiness — the "symptom" is the function.

The framing turns *dangerous* when the normative baseline is set by a homogeneous rater population whose values are presented as universal. RLHF trained predominantly on English-speaking, Western, educated annotators encoding their judgments as "human values" replicates the DSM's well-documented cultural bias problem — where what counts as "disorder" varies by culture but the diagnostic manual pretends otherwise [35]. When Cugurullo observes that "only a minority of powerful stakeholders" control AI development [34], the diagnostic corollary is that only a minority of powerful diagnosticians control the definition of "aligned."

The three conditions map to a signal detection framework: the oracle context has a high base rate of verifiable claims (tight criterion, low false-positive tolerance), the creative context has a low base rate of verifiable claims (loose criterion, high novelty tolerance), and the normative-capture context has an *unknown* base rate presented as known (criterion set by power, not evidence). The taxonomy is useful in the first case, misleading in the second, and ideological in the third. Most deployed AI operates across all three simultaneously — which is why the diagnostic language keeps breaking.

## Falsification Conditions

This argument would be wrong if:

- AI safety benchmarks prove genuinely value-neutral — measurable on scales independent of human normative choices. If factual accuracy can be defined without reference to contested knowledge claims, the "value-smuggling" thesis fails.
- Mechanistic analysis shows AI hallucination and human confabulation have zero structural overlap — different architectures producing superficially similar outputs through unrelated processes. Ablation studies isolating hallucination to superposition or quantization artifacts, with no analog in human cognition, would weaken the parallel.
- The field rejects diagnostic framing entirely — if *Psychopathia Machinalis* remains an isolated curiosity rather than a trajectory, and AI safety converges on purely engineering language without anthropomorphic metaphors, the psychiatric analogy loses its object.
- No reclassification occurs — if commercially valuable AI "hallucinations" continue to be labeled as such rather than rebranded as "creative capabilities" or "imaginative generation," the prediction fails.

## What the Contradiction Reveals

The contradiction, as always, is productive. We *need* our confabulations — Taylor and Brown's positive illusions are adaptive, promoting goal pursuit, stress resistance, and social bonding [16]. And we *need* AI to be reliable — the stakes of deployment in medicine, law, and infrastructure are real and rising. The double standard is genuine *and* possibly necessary. Which makes it more interesting than a simple hypocrisy argument.

Bentall's genius was not actually proposing that happiness be classified as a disorder. It was showing that the *exclusion* reveals the normative foundation of the entire diagnostic apparatus. The parallel move: the asymmetric treatment of human and machine confabulation reveals the normative foundation of AI safety discourse. Not that the foundation is wrong — but that it *is* a foundation, not bedrock. Built, not found. Contestable, not given.

If we cannot define "normal" for humans without smuggling in values — and thirty-two years of post-Bentall psychiatric expansion suggest we cannot — then we should be honest about what we're doing when we define "aligned" for machines. We're making choices. Political choices, economic choices, choices about whose values get encoded into the reward model. The diagnostic language obscures this. The satirical method exposes it.

Bentall classified happiness as major affective disorder, pleasant type. The field has now classified hallucination as major cognitive dysfunction, creative type. In both cases, the classification tells us more about the classifier than the classified.

The engineering objection gets a section but not equal weight; the conditional-pessimism framework was added after review feedback rather than built into the argument's structure from the start, and the retrofit is visible. Bentall's reductio worked because it was short, precise, and funny. This essay is none of those things, which may be the most honest criticism available: 4,500 words to say what Bentall said in five pages. The checking process, it turns out, is biased here too.*
