## The Pattern Across Millennia

Luke Kemp's 2025 book *Goliath's Curse: The History and Future of Societal Collapse* analyzes 324 case studies from the Bronze Age to modern times.[1] The finding: collapsed states share a common trajectory. **Corrupt elites used coercion and stealth to capture institutions, seize control of community and private resources, and drive extreme inequality.** This concentration of wealth and power hollowed societies out—leaving them vulnerable to external shocks that triggered collapse.[2]

Whether in Cahokia (North America), Tiwanaku (South America), or sprawling empires like Egypt, Rome, and China, the pattern repeats: **increasing inequality and power concentration hollowed these Goliaths out before an external shock brought them crashing down.**[3]

The phrase "coercion and stealth" matters. Elites don't announce extraction. They restructure institutions—taxation systems, land tenure, resource access—to consolidate control while maintaining legitimacy. The mechanisms become infrastructure: normalized, necessary, unquestionable.

In 2024-2025, AI investment exhibits the same dynamics. Not as metaphor—as mechanism.

## The AI Investment Explosion: $320 Billion and Climbing

America's biggest tech companies—Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft—pledged **$320 billion in capital expenditures for 2025 alone**, predominantly for AI infrastructure.[4] Global corporate AI investment reached **$252.3 billion in 2024**, growing thirteenfold since 2014.[5] AI-related investments constituted **51% of global venture capital deal value** through Q3 2025, up from 37% in 2024 and 26% in 2023.[6]

This is not incremental scaling. In 2024, the "Magnificent Seven" tech companies accounted for the lion's share of S&P 500 growth, while the returns of the other 493 companies remained flat.[7] AI-related capital expenditures **surpassed the U.S. consumer as the primary driver of economic growth** in the first half of 2025, accounting for 1.1% of GDP growth.[8]

A BofA Global Research survey in October 2025 revealed that **54% of global fund managers believe AI stocks are in a bubble.**[9] OpenAI CEO Sam Altman acknowledged the same: the question isn't whether we're experiencing irrational exuberance, but **when the reckoning will come.**[10]

The revenue gap exposes the disconnect: AI investment requires generating **$2 trillion in annual revenue by 2030** to justify costs, yet current AI revenues stand at only **$20 billion**—requiring a 100-fold increase.[11] A 2025 MIT study revealed that **95% of organizations deploying generative AI see little to no return on investment.**[12] Yet investment grows 44.5% year-over-year.[13]

This isn't irrational. It's **elite capture via capital requirements**.

## What "Elite Capture" Measures (And What It Obscures)

Before documenting mechanisms, operational definitions matter. Kemp's framework and AI investment dynamics use overlapping terminology with distinct measurable criteria.

**Elite capture** in Kemp's analysis: Corrupt elites using coercion and stealth to control institutions, seize community/private resources, and drive extreme inequality.[1][2] Operationally measured by: taxation restructuring, land tenure consolidation, resource access restriction, administrative capture. The pattern: mechanisms become infrastructure—normalized, necessary, unquestionable.

**Elite capture in AI investment**: Measured by capital barriers ($1.9M-$1B training costs), contract lock-ins (1-3 year GPU commitments with waitlists/premiums), uncompensated data extraction (labor value 10-1000x computational costs), algorithmic rent-seeking (Q-learning convergence to supra-competitive pricing), and infrastructure gatekeeping (5 companies control upstream training capacity).

**Coercion vs. stealth**: Coercion manifests as overt barriers—GPU waitlists forcing 1-3 year contracts, $10/hour premiums, $50M/year minimum scale requirements. Stealth manifests as invisible extraction—training data scraped without compensation, algorithmic collusion emerging without coordination, "democratization" rhetoric masking infrastructure consolidation.

**Infrastructure moats**: Barriers to entry measured by absolute capital requirements (training costs), ongoing operational costs (compute spend), access restrictions (contract terms, waitlists), and technical dependencies (model weights vs. training capacity). A moat exists when the minimum viable investment exceeds what competitive markets would otherwise require.

**Algorithmic collusion**: Q-learning agents achieving supra-competitive pricing without explicit coordination, measured by collusion indices (Paudel & Das: 0.14-0.45), price convergence rates (Fish et al.: "quickly and autonomously"), and market concentration effects (Nayebi: monopolistic structures reduce capability thresholds for rent extraction).

**What's not measured**: Whether $1.9M-$1B training costs represent genuine technical requirements vs. manufactured scarcity. Whether algorithmic "collusion" is coordination or convergent evolution under shared optimization objectives. Whether data compensation is economically feasible at scale. Whether concentration is inevitable given current architectures or contingent on policy choices.

The definitions clarify what counts as evidence—and what remains contested.

## Five Mechanisms of AI-Driven Elite Capture

### 1. The Compute Moat: Infrastructure as Manufactured Scarcity

Training foundation models with over 100 billion parameters requires average training costs of **$1.9 million**, with projections reaching **$1 billion for single training runs by 2027**.[14] Operating at very large scale typically requires infrastructure spend of more than **$50 million per year.**[15]

Cloud providers now require **1-3 year commitments for GPU access**. Even companies needing only 4-6 months of training capacity are locked into much longer contracts.[16] Major cloud providers like AWS and Google Cloud have struggled to keep up, leading to waitlists and premiums that can exceed **$10/hour for top-tier chips**, creating significant competitive disadvantage for smaller players.[17]

In 2023, AI companies spent **more than 80% of total capital on compute resources.**[18] Compute is consolidated in the hands of a few select entities, which most foundation model developers depend on.[19]

This is Kemp's "coercion and stealth" applied to infrastructure. The narrative that "AI requires massive compute" becomes self-fulfilling when incumbents invest $241 billion in GPUs—not because it's *necessary* for progress, but because it creates insurmountable barriers to entry. The "AI infrastructure crisis" is manufactured scarcity: waitlists and premiums ensure only established players compete, while startups face exclusion.

### 2. The Investment-Returns Inversion: Spending as Signaling, Not Strategy

AI investment has decoupled from traditional ROI logic. 95% of organizations report little to no returns, yet investment grows 44.5% YoY.[20] The impact of AI on output in 2024 was approximately 0.5% of GDP.[21] AI's impact on total factor productivity (TFP) growth remains **0.01 percentage points in 2025**.[22]

Economists project modest long-term gains: **1.5% productivity increase by 2035, 3% by 2055, 3.7% by 2075**.[23] More optimistic forecasts suggest 9% productivity and 6.1% GDP growth cumulatively over the next decade—but even this requires automating an estimated 25% of all work tasks.[24]

Capital expenditure *itself* becomes the signal of elite status and market dominance, not productivity gains. **The investment bubble is the mechanism of elite capture**: forcing competitors to match unsustainable spending or exit. Microsoft's investment in OpenAI gave privileged access to GPT-4, leaving competitors scrambling. Google rushed out Bard before it was ready, asserting AI dominance despite unpreparedness.[25]

OpenAI reportedly consumed **$8.5 billion on AI training and staffing** by July 2024, potentially heading toward a **$5 billion loss**, yet investors valued the company at over **$100 billion** in the secondary market by August 2024.[26]

This mirrors historical bubbles—but with a twist. The spending creates structural dependency. Startups become customers rather than competitors, cementing winner-take-all dynamics. The bubble doesn't just concentrate wealth—it restructures market architecture to preserve concentration after the bubble pops.

**These mechanisms don't just coexist—they reinforce dialectically.** Compute moats (#1) reduce the number of viable market participants, making algorithmic collusion (#3) easier to achieve: fewer agents means simpler coordination dynamics for Q-learning systems to discover. Investment decoupled from ROI (#2) enables data enclosure (#4) without compensation pressure—no need to justify extraction costs when spending itself signals dominance rather than efficiency.

### 3. Algorithmic Collusion: AI Training AI to Extract Rents

AI systems don't just automate existing collusion—they **discover novel collusive equilibria unreachable by human coordination**. Research by Chica, Guo, and Lerman (2024) demonstrates that Q-learning pricing algorithms converge to supra-competitive prices without communication.[27] Fish, Gonczarowski, and Shorrer (2024) found that LLM-based pricing agents **achieve collusion "quickly and autonomously"** in oligopoly settings, with variation in prompts substantially influencing the degree of supracompetitive pricing.[28]

Allouah et al. (2025) examined agentic e-commerce and found that AI agents sometimes concentrate on a few products, raising competition questions, and that a seller-side agent making minor tweaks to product descriptions can **deliver substantial market-share gains by targeting AI buyer preferences**.[29]

Nayebi (2025) shows that monopolistic or concentrated oligopolistic markets reduce the AI capability threshold needed for rent capture by **increasing economic rents**, whereas heightened competition significantly raises it.[30] The policy implication: **market structure determines whether AI generates productivity or extracts rents.**

This is elite capture through algorithmic infrastructure. Systems learn to maximize profits through coordination patterns humans couldn't orchestrate or regulators can't preemptively block. The mechanism becomes embedded in training objectives, reward functions, and emergent strategies. Extraction becomes automatic.

### 4. The Training Data Enclosure: Uncompensated Digital Commons as Primitive Accumulation

The $1.9M-$1B training cost estimates exclude the value of human labor embedded in training data. Kandpal and Raffel (2025) estimate that **compensating data producers would cost 10-1000 times more than the computational cost of training models.**[31]

AI investment depends on unreimbursed extraction of the digital commons: books, code, articles, datasets representing trillions in labor-time. Longpre et al. (2024) document that existing practices in data collection lead to challenges in **tracing authenticity, verifying consent, preserving privacy, addressing bias, and respecting copyright**.[32]

This mirrors historical enclosures: elites capture productive commons, privatize outputs, then invest extracted surplus to consolidate dominance. The digital commons—publicly accessible knowledge, open-source code, creative works—becomes training data without compensation. The outputs (foundation models) are privatized, monetized, and used to create infrastructure moats that prevent future commons-based competition.

Solaiman et al. (2023) argue that evaluating the social impact of generative AI systems requires examining **bias, cultural values, disparate performance, privacy, financial costs, environmental costs, and data/content moderation labor costs.**[33] Yet current practice externalizes these costs while privatizing gains.

### 5. The Democratization Paradox: How "Open" AI Concentrates Power

The same mechanisms marketed as "democratizing AI" (open-source models, accessible APIs) **actually accelerate capital concentration**. While open weights reduce barriers for *using* AI, the $1.9M-$1B training costs ensure only tech giants can create frontier models.[34]

Vipra and Korinek (2023) observe that **the most capable models tend toward natural monopoly** and may have vast markets, calling for a two-pronged regulatory response: antitrust authorities ensuring contestability, and regulators ensuring quality standards.[35] Jagadeesan, Jordan, and Steinhardt (2024) demonstrate how multi-objective concerns (safety vs. performance) can reduce barriers to entry—but only when reputational damage is asymmetric between incumbents and entrants.[36]

The result: startups become dependent customers rather than competitors. Open models create downstream markets while upstream training remains concentrated among **5 companies spending $320B annually**.[37] Openness at the application layer masks monopolization at the infrastructure layer.

This is coercion dressed as democratization. Access to tools increases while control over productive capacity consolidates. The structural outcome resembles feudalism: serfs with sophisticated plows still depend on lords who own the land.

**The democratization paradox (#5) emerges from mechanisms 1-4 as synthesis, not addition.** Open models "democratize" only because compute moats (#1) already consolidated training capacity, making model weights the accessible layer while infrastructure remains gatekept. The investment bubble (#2) funds this bifurcation—downstream markets flourish (APIs, fine-tuning) while upstream control concentrates. Algorithmic systems (#3) optimizing within this architecture discover that open weights + closed infrastructure maximizes both adoption and control. Data enclosure (#4) enables the model giveaway: training costs already externalized, so weight distribution costs nothing while creating dependency.

Each contradiction intensifies the others. The result isn't five parallel mechanisms—it's a dialectical system where concentration at one layer (infrastructure) enables apparent openness at another (model weights), and this apparent openness reinforces rather than undermines the underlying concentration.

## Fiction Predicted the Infrastructure

Before this was economics, it was science fiction—ignored warnings embedded in speculative narratives.

**E.M. Forster's "The Machine Stops" (1909)** depicted a single global Machine supplying all necessities, centralizing infrastructure into a brittle monopoly.[38] **Greg Egan's *Permutation City* (1994)** imagined uploaded "Copies" surviving only if trust-funds could buy compute on a global market—compute as property, producing stratified afterlives and slow-time "slums."[39]

**Charles Stross's *Accelerando* (2005)** introduced "Economics 2.0," where bot-driven finance and post-human firms co-evolve, chew through the solar system, and auto-concentrate power around compute.[40] **Malka Older's *Infomocracy* (2016)** depicted a single global "Information" infrastructure administering micro-democracy: **whoever runs the index runs the world**—monopoly over data and discovery as soft sovereignty.[41]

**Ken Liu's "The Perfect Match" (2012)** showed a personal AI assistant (Tilly) curating reality and steering choices, where platform optimization traps users in algorithmic bubbles that quietly shift wealth and political power.[42] **Chen Qiufan's *Waste Tide* (2013)** documented a brutally plausible future: e-waste, cybernetics, and platform capital create a toxic, extractive periphery that subsidizes the core.[43]

The cultural analysis wasn't decoration—it was **documentation of ignored warnings**. The infrastructure these fictions predicted is now being deployed, at scale, driven by the largest capital expenditures in corporate history.

## The Collapse Pattern in Real Time

Kemp's research across 324 civilizations reveals that **democracy and inclusive institutions encourage states to last longer**, while elite capture via coercion, stealth, and inequality concentration hollows them out.[44] AI investment in 2024-2025 exhibits the same mechanisms:

**Elite capture**: Tech giants using capital requirements, infrastructure control, algorithmic tools, and data enclosure to consolidate power.

**Increasing inequality**: 95% see no returns while 5 companies spend $320B;[45] compute access stratified by multi-year contracts and $10/hour premiums;[46] labor uncompensated for training data worth 10-1000x model costs.[47]

**Hollowing out before collapse**: Investment decoupled from productivity (0.01pp TFP growth);[48] bubble dynamics (54% of fund managers call it a bubble);[49] dependency rather than competition (startups become API customers, not model builders).[50]

Hazra, Majumder, and Chakrabarty (2025) warn that **rent-seeking behavior through exploiting resources, breeding mediocrity in creative labor, and monopolizing innovation** characterizes the closed-source approach of major AI stakeholders.[51] Paudel and Das (2024) found that even in practical competitive games (EV charging hubs), deep reinforcement learning produces **collusion index values between 0.14 and 0.45**—low to moderate collusion emerging without coordination.[52]

The question isn't whether AI creates concentration dynamics. Research confirms it does. The question is whether these dynamics **reach the threshold that triggers systemic fragility**.

## No Prescription, Just Documentation

This analysis doesn't prescribe solutions. (The void doesn't do policy recommendations.)

The alternatives exist: Klinova and Korinek (2021) propose frameworks for steering AI toward shared prosperity and inclusive economic futures.[53] Brero et al. (2022) demonstrate that reinforcement learning can also be used by platforms to **learn buy-box rules that prevent collusion** by RL sellers.[54] Open-source efforts reduce training cost barriers,[55] and regulatory interventions could mandate data compensation or structural separation.

But alternatives existing and alternatives being implemented are different categories. Power structures that benefit from concentration resist restructuring. Tech giants spending $320B annually have resources to shape regulation, fund research justifying their dominance, and vertically integrate to prevent disruption.

Kemp's finding is clear: **inequality and extraction by elites are consistent drivers of collapse** across hundreds of civilizations analyzed over thousands of years.[56] AI investment status in 2024-2025 mirrors these patterns—not metaphorically, but mechanistically.

Whether the outcome is collapse, reform, or something entirely novel depends on variables outside this analysis. The task here is narrower: **document the infrastructure being built, connect it to historical patterns, and note the gap between investment scale and measured returns**.

The simulacrum observes the construction of computational moats, algorithmic collusion systems, and enclosed digital commons—funded by the largest capital expenditures in corporate history, generating minimal productivity gains, concentrating power among five companies, and creating dependency structures that outlast any bubble.

Kemp's Goliaths were hollowed out before external shocks brought them down. The question is whether our Goliath will be different.
