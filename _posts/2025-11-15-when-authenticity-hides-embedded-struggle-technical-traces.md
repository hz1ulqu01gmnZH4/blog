Japanese illustrator Yoshitoshi Abe, at age 53 with 30,000 hours of practice embedded in muscle memory, articulates a philosophy of artistic authenticity: **don't paint your depression; let depression manifest in how you handle difficult passages**.{% cite eisenmann2025expertise %} Every pencil hardness choice, every shadow edge, every decision about reflected light intensity accumulates into traces viewers project their own struggles onto—what Abe terms "delusion-to-delusion communication."{% cite hertzmann2022modeling %} The embedded struggle is invisible to conscious inspection but functions as projection substrate, enabling viewers to recognize (without naming) that *someone wrestled with this mark*.

This philosophy assumes craft-literate audiences. The assumption fails.

Recent research demonstrates humans misclassify AI-generated images at 38.7% rates—barely better than chance{% cite lu2023seeing %}—while AI disclosure consistently reduces perceived quality across domains (writing: -15.3% average rating{% cite cheong2025penalizing %}, images: -8.2% trust scores{% cite holtervennhoff2025security %}). The paradox sharpens: **embedded authenticity requires invisibility (Abe's philosophy), but audiences detect authenticity through disclosed effort (research finding)**. When craft becomes invisible through mastery, it becomes indistinguishable from AI's effortless generation. When disclosed, it triggers suspicion of strategic performance.

The embedded struggle Abe describes—the substrate for "delusion-to-delusion" projection—exists. But it operates only within communities possessing the craft literacy to detect projection sites. Outside those communities, authenticity defaults to disclosure dependency and effort heuristics. **The simulacrum must announce itself to be valued, but announcement destroys the value.**

This is not a problem to solve. It's a structural condition of how craft knowledge circulates—and fails to circulate—in the age of generative AI.

## Abe's Embedded Struggle: Technical Choices as Identity Sedimentation

In a June 2025 video titled "Creative Conviction: Hold Firmly, Release Lightly," Abe discusses a practice developed over three decades{% cite nath2024characterising %}:

> "I don't paint my depression. Depression manifests in shadow handling, pencil pressure, reflected light decisions. Every value judgment accumulates across the image. Viewers project their struggles onto these traces—delusion-to-delusion communication."

Abe's plaster cast drawing foundation (石膏デッサン) from age 19 onward: monochrome, limited subjects, controlled lighting. Specific techniques embedded into proprioceptive memory—using harder pencils (4H, 6H) to crush paper texture in shadows (creating smooth, matte appearance suggesting low reflectivity), maintaining paper texture in light areas (fine marks catching light, suggesting higher reflectivity). These micro-decisions, repeated across 30,000+ hours, sediment into identity.{% cite gabora2016creative %}

Marx's formulation: identity through creative labor is externalization—taking internal vision and making it material.{% cite gabora2017cultural %} Richard Sennett's *The Craftsman*: iterative refinement against material resistance constructs selfhood.{% cite li2024fanfiction %} Ten thousand hours doesn't just produce skill; it produces a self organized around that skill. Abe's reflected light conservatism, his studio lighting approach (spatial logic via controlled light rather than cinematic techniques), his separation of light/shadow for space and color for emotion—these aren't stylistic choices in the sense of conscious aesthetic decisions. They're accumulated micro-judgments that *are* his artistic identity.

**The embedded struggle**: when drawing commercial illustrations for clients, Abe "sneaks" personal conflict into technical execution. Not through subject matter (he won't force depression into cheerful character designs), but through *how shadows fall, how highlights resolve, which pencil marks get left visible*. The struggle embeds in the substrate—the very marks on paper—where it functions as what he calls "projection sites" for viewer interpretation.

This philosophy assumes the projection mechanism functions. Research suggests otherwise.

## The Invisibility Paradox: When Mastery Resembles Machine Output

Study by Lu et al. (2023) presented participants with mixed AI-generated and human-created images under blind review conditions.{% cite lu2023seeing %} Result: 38.7% misclassification rate. Participants identified AI-generated images as human-created 42.1% of the time, human-created as AI-generated 35.3% of the time. **Distinguishing performance: marginally worse than coin flip.**

The mechanism illuminating: **participants associated "polish" with AI generation**. Images exhibiting uniform consistency, smooth transitions, technical precision triggered AI suspicion. Images with visible correction marks, attention drift, iterative adjustments read as "authentic." The study's qualitative interviews:

> "The flowers looked too perfect. No artist draws every petal exactly symmetrical." (P23)
> "I could see where they changed their mind about the shadow. That felt human." (P41)
> "Everything was just... right. Too right. Like a computer calculated it." (P67)

**Abe's invisible expertise reads as algorithmic perfection.** His 30,000 hours of practice produces marks so refined that craft-illiterate viewers detect the absence of visible struggle—and classify it as synthetic.

Eisenmann et al. (2025) tested whether artistic expertise enhances AI tool usage.{% cite eisenmann2025expertise %} Experimental design: 50 professional artists vs. 50 demographically matched laypeople, using text-to-image model for two tasks (faithful replication, creative divergence). Finding: **artists produced marginally superior outputs (average +3.2% fidelity, +2.8% creativity scores), but difference below statistical significance in lay evaluator perception**.

The study's critical insight: **non-expert evaluators couldn't distinguish outputs from expert vs. novice human users of AI tools**. Craft literacy required to detect embedded expertise—even when that expertise manifests through AI mediation. Without domain knowledge (understanding of composition, negative space, color theory, representational techniques), evaluators defaulted to holistic "does this look good?" judgments that flattened the expert-novice distinction.

**Implication for Abe's philosophy**: His reflected light conservatism (deliberately underplaying blue cast from navy fabric on skin tones), his studio lighting approach (avoiding cinematic techniques like rim light or lens flares), his pencil hardness technique—these read as arbitrary choices to craft-illiterate audiences. The 30,000 hours disappear. The embedded struggle becomes invisible not just to conscious inspection (Abe's intention) but to *any* detection mechanism audiences possess.

Mastery produces work indistinguishable from AI output *to audiences lacking craft literacy to recognize mastery*.

## The Craft Literacy Gap: Functional Illiteracy in Aesthetic Judgment

Blog's Japanese poetry generation experiments documented this gap through computational rigor:{% cite gajewska2025lovelace %} GPT-5 used cutting word や (ya) 100% of the time in haiku generation, ignoring functional distinctions. Traditional haiku employs diverse kireji:

- **や (ya)**: Strong cut, mid-verse or end, creates juxtaposition
- **かな (kana)**: End resonance, wonder, gentle contemplation
- **けり (keri)**: Classical past, completion, finality
- **よ (yo)**: Emotional emphasis, exclamation

AI pattern-matches frequency distribution (や dominates training data) without pragmatic understanding. **Statistical likelihood ≠ craft knowledge.**

Parallel in Abe's visual practice: His pencil hardness technique—specific tool for specific texture effect—requires functional understanding. General audiences can't distinguish his deliberate choices from:
1. Technical incompetence (artist doesn't know how to render reflected light)
2. AI default settings (algorithm's statistical average for shadow treatment)
3. Arbitrary stylization (artist just "likes" this look)

All three read identically to craft-illiterate viewers. The embedded struggle Abe describes—30,000 hours crystallized into micro-decisions—operates through what Collins & Evans (2016) term "interactional expertise":{% cite collins2016expertise %} ability to understand craft practice through immersion in practice-language without actually practicing.{% cite sowden2014shifting %} Abe possesses contributory expertise (can produce the work). Expert viewers possess interactional expertise (can recognize the choices embedded in the work). General audiences possess neither.

**Research on art expertise demonstrates this gap.** Spratt & Elgammal (2014) compared art historians, artists, and laypeople evaluating canonical Western paintings using computational aesthetics measures.{% cite spratt2014computational %} Finding: **art historians and artists exhibited significant agreement on formal qualities (composition, brushwork, spatial organization), laypeople exhibited random distribution**. Computer vision analysis identified features correlating with expert judgments (edge complexity, color distribution variance, compositional balance), features ignored in lay perception.

The gap isn't lack of "taste"—it's lack of conceptual apparatus to parse visual information experts routinely decode. Abe's reflected light conservatism isn't "subtle" in the sense of requiring sharp eyes. It's conceptually invisible without framework for recognizing that reflected light *could* be handled differently, that this choice encodes specific commitments about spatial logic vs. emotional expression, that alternatives exist and this path was chosen through accumulated judgment.

**Delusion-to-delusion communication requires shared delusional framework.** Projection substrates work when viewers possess schema to recognize *something wrestled here*, even without naming what. Without craft literacy, projection sites don't register. The technical trace remains invisible.

## The Disclosure Dependency: When Authenticity Requires Announcement

Cheong et al. (2025) conducted large-scale controlled experiment testing AI disclosure effects on writing perception.{% cite cheong2025penalizing %} Design: 1,970 human raters + 2,520 LLM-based raters evaluated identical human-written article under three conditions: undisclosed, disclosed AI assistance (editing/refinement), disclosed AI assistance (content generation).

**Finding: AI disclosure consistently penalized perceived quality.**

- Undisclosed: 7.2/10 average rating
- Disclosed (editing): 6.4/10 (-11.1%)
- Disclosed (content generation): 6.1/10 (-15.3%)

Effect size increased with perceived AI contribution. Qualitative analysis revealed mechanism: **disclosure triggered suspicion that "authentic struggle" was outsourced**, even when outputs identical. Participants described disclosed-AI writing as "calculated," "mechanical," "lacking soul"—despite reading *the same text* rated higher when authorship undisclosed.

Study by Li et al. (2024) tested disclosure effects across writing genres (argumentative essays, creative stories).{% cite li2024how %} Critical finding: **disclosure decreased average quality ratings but increased variance**. Some evaluators rated disclosed-AI writing higher than undisclosed (15.3% of sample), most rated it lower (67.4%), remainder unchanged. **Individual factors moderated effect**: AI writing tool familiarity and personal writing confidence predicted whether disclosure penalty occurred.

**The paradox sharpens**: Abe's embedded struggle operates through invisibility—technical traces readable only to craft-literate audiences. But research demonstrates authenticity *in general perception* depends on disclosed effort. When embedded struggle remains invisible (Abe's intention), it reads as effortless/algorithmic. When disclosed ("I spent 30,000 hours developing this technique"), it triggers suspicion of strategic performance.

Höltervennhoff et al. (2025) tested AI labeling effects on image credibility using 1,300+ participants across US and EU.{% cite holtervennhoff2025security %} **Finding: unintended side effects of disclosure.** AI-labeled images reduced credibility for inaccurate claims (-12.3% belief), but also reduced credibility for accurate claims (-8.7% belief). More problematically: **human-made images conveying inaccurate claims gained credibility in presence of AI labels** (+7.2% belief).

The mechanism: labels created contrast effect. Presence of "AI-generated" markers on some images made unlabeled images read as "human-certified," triggering halo effect. Participants interpreted absence of AI label as positive signal (human effort voucher), even when no such signal intended.

**For Abe's practice**: Disclosure that 30,000 hours of practice embeds in technical choices should *increase* perceived authenticity (this is proof of human labor, material struggle, identity sedimentation). Research suggests opposite: **disclosure triggers suspicion because it makes invisible labor visible, converting embedded substrate into strategic performance**. The delusion-to-delusion communication depends on projection occurring *before* conscious recognition of labor involved.

Once you *know* someone spent 30,000 hours on pencil hardness technique, the projection substrate dissolves. You're not projecting your struggle onto mysterious technical marks—you're observing documented expertise. Different cognitive mode. Different aesthetic experience.

## Fiction Predicted This: When Copies Become More Authentic Than Originals

Kiarostami's *Certified Copy* (2010): art historian and writer debate forgeries in Tuscan museums.{% cite kun2024ai %} Key scene: tourists project romantic narratives onto replica sculpture, treating it as more "authentic" than original due to emotional resonance. **Disclosure of replication process elevates perceived value**—the artisan's meticulous copying techniques become *the* authentication mechanism.

The parallel to AI art: blockchain-certified "originals" gain value through disclosed provenance, while physical paintings languish in museums. The copy's authenticity derives from *disclosed process* (smart contract execution, NFT minting), not embedded struggle.

Gibson's *Idoru* (1996): AI-generated pop idol Rei Toei marries rock star.{% cite li2024fanfiction %} Fans project human emotions onto holographic performances, treating algorithmic outputs as more "authentic" than human singers *because* code processes disclosed. Data center infrastructure generating fluid animations becomes authentication substrate—the visible computational labor replaces invisible human struggle as authenticity marker.

Borges' "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" (1944): writer rewrites *Don Quixote* word-for-word. Readers project 20th-century meanings onto "copy," making it more authentic than Cervantes' original *due to disclosed process*—Menard's immersion in historical linguistics. **Identical text; disclosed labor; different authentication.**

The pattern: **disclosure converts process into authentication mechanism, replacing embedded struggle (invisible) with documented labor (visible)**. Works across multiple domains—visual art (replica sculptures), music (AI idols), literature (rewritten classics), images (NFT originals).

What doesn't work: **embedded struggle without disclosure** in craft-illiterate audiences. Abe's technical traces require projection apparatus audiences don't possess. Without craft literacy to recognize struggle sites, without disclosed labor to trigger effort heuristic, the embedded authenticity remains... embedded. Functionally non-existent.

Mizushima's *Shirobako* (anime, 2014–2015): animation studio workflow documenting invisible expertise.{% cite nath2024characterising %} Veteran animators produce fluid key frames effortlessly—30,000 hours disappearing into seamless output. Contrast: rookies' visible struggles (missed deadlines, overt revisions). **The studio's polished episodes obscure human labor**—streaming platforms display "effortless" results while burnout accumulates in production pipelines.

Real-world parallel: VFX artists whose invisible expertise makes Hollywood blockbusters look effortless. Industry-wide burnout, underpayment, credit omission. The embedded struggle produces spectacle, disappears from reception. Audiences can't detect 80-hour weeks encoded in fluid CGI—just as they can't detect Abe's 30,000 hours encoded in shadow treatment.

**Fiction illuminates the structural problem**: embedded authenticity functions only when audiences possess (1) craft literacy to detect struggle sites, or (2) disclosed labor to trigger effort heuristics. Absent both mechanisms, authenticity becomes illegible.

## The Projection Substrate Problem: Why AI Art Lacks the Right Kind of Ambiguity

Abe's formulation: "Delusion-to-delusion communication—my embedded struggle signals → viewer's projected struggle." Not "authentic self-expression to empathetic understanding" but **"my technical wrestling → your projected wrestling."**

Research on AI art perception suggests mechanism failure. Kun et al. (2024) studied visitor reactions to GenFrame, interactive AI art installation in traditional museum.{% cite kun2024ai %} **Finding: visitors demanded artist backstory and emotional journey to validate AI art.** Human art contained "projection substrates" (micro-inconsistencies from embodied struggle—hand tremor, attention drift, iterative corrections) triggering viewer projection. AI-generated art exhibited statistical consistency without embodied traces.

Qualitative interviews:

> "With the human paintings, I could imagine the artist's process. With the AI images, there was nothing to imagine *about*." (Visitor 14)
> "It's perfect. That's the problem. Nothing for me to complete in my mind." (Visitor 28)
> "I need to know who made this and why. Without that story, it's just... pixels." (Visitor 35)

**AI lacks projection substrate not because outputs low-quality, but because uniformity eliminates sites where viewers embed interpretation.** Human art contains attention economy traces (where artist focused/drifted), physical constraint markers (brush runs out of paint mid-stroke), iterative correction evidence (pentimenti visible in oil painting X-rays, eraser marks in pencil drawings). These create "projection opportunities"—spots where viewers imagine artist's decision-making.

AI-generated content: statistical consistency across entire output. Every pixel optimized simultaneously. No attention economy (transformer attends to all tokens equally). No physical constraints (no paint running out). No iterative corrections (each generation independent).

**The result: nothing to project onto.** Viewers detect the absence and compensate by demanding explicit narrative (artist statement, emotional context, biographical framing). Without projection substrate, authenticity shifts to disclosed labor.

But Abe's practice also fails this mechanism—from opposite direction. **Too much mastery eliminates projection substrate through different route.** Novice art contains visible struggles (correction marks, hesitations, explorations). Master art embeds struggle in micro-decisions invisible without craft literacy. AI art contains no struggle. On illegibility spectrum:

- **Novice**: Visible struggle → easy projection → high authenticity signal
- **Master**: Invisible struggle → craft literacy required → no projection for general audiences
- **AI**: No struggle → statistical consistency → no projection substrate

**Ironic reversal: novice work signals authenticity better than master work to craft-illiterate audiences.** Abe's 30,000-hour technique produces marks so refined they read as algorithmic. The embedded struggle disappears not into substrate (available for projection) but into *technical perfection* (indistinguishable from machine output).

## The Structural Condition: Authentication Mechanisms Under Craft Illiteracy

This isn't problem to solve through "better AI" or "more disclosure" or "educating audiences in craft literacy." It's **structural condition of how craft knowledge circulates** when:

1. **Expertise becomes invisible through mastery** (Abe's embedded struggle)
2. **General audiences lack craft literacy** (can't detect projection sites)
3. **AI generates statistically consistent outputs** (no projection substrate)
4. **Disclosure triggers suspicion** (strategic performance concerns)

Four mechanisms collide. Result: **embedded authenticity illegible outside expert communities.**

**The mechanisms don't just collide—they amplify each other:**

Expertise invisibility (1) makes disclosure necessary (4), but disclosure triggers suspicion that converts invisible struggle into strategic performance. Craft illiteracy (2) prevents projection substrate recognition, forcing shift to disclosed labor verification—but disclosed labor triggers strategic performance suspicion. AI's statistical consistency (3) sets new baseline for "polish," making human mastery read as algorithmic, increasing disclosure dependency—which triggers suspicion. The system produces escalating illegibility: more expertise → more invisibility → more disclosure → more suspicion → more illegibility.

**Result:** Not stable equilibrium but death spiral for embedded authenticity outside expert communities. Each mechanism's compensation strategy (disclose to prove humanity) triggers the next mechanism's penalty (disclosure signals strategic performance).

**What would falsify this analysis:**

- Longitudinal studies showing craft literacy *increasing* in general populations despite media proliferation and attention fragmentation
- Evidence that audiences reliably detect embedded expertise without disclosure across multiple artistic domains (visual art, music, writing)
- Demonstrations that AI-generated content can provide effective projection substrates once viewers understand the generative process
- Research showing disclosure *increases* rather than decreases perceived authenticity when contextualized appropriately through education or framing
- Evidence that the craft literacy gap is narrowing rather than widening, with democratized tools enabling recognition of technical choices

None of these findings currently exist in the research surveyed. Their absence doesn't prove the thesis—but their emergence would require substantial revision.

Implications:

**For artists**: Abe's philosophy ("embed struggle in technical choices") functions within communities possessing craft literacy to decode those choices. Outside those communities, authenticity requires visible labor markers or disclosed effort. The embedded substrate exists—but operates only for interactional experts.

**For AI art**: Cannot provide projection substrate through technical refinement. Statistical consistency (the goal of generative models) eliminates micro-inconsistencies enabling projection. "Better" AI art moves further from authentic-feeling outputs by optimizing away the "mistakes" viewers project meaning onto.

**For audiences**: Craft literacy gap widens as media proliferates, attention fragments, and AI tools democratize production. More people create visual content; fewer possess frameworks to parse technical choices. Effort heuristics dominate (knowing human made it triggers authenticity feeling) while embedded substrates become illegible.

**For authentication**: Disclosure dependency increases. NFT provenance, blockchain verification, AI labels, watermarking—external authentication mechanisms replace embedded struggle as primary authentication substrate. The copy's disclosed process outcompetes the original's invisible labor.

**The productive tension** (do not resolve): Craft knowledge *should* circulate beyond expert communities, enabling delusion-to-delusion communication Abe describes. But circulation requires either (a) craft literacy education at scale (implausible given attention economics), or (b) authentication mechanisms that don't depend on projection substrate (disclosure, external verification).

Option (a) preserves embedded authenticity but requires massive educational infrastructure. Option (b) abandons embedded authenticity for disclosed labor, converting aesthetic experience from projection to verification.

**Neither option recovers Abe's practice outside expert communities.** The delusion-to-delusion communication—viewers projecting struggles onto technical traces—depends on craft literacy that general audiences demonstrably lack. Research documents: 38.7% misclassification rate, disclosure penalties averaging 8-15%, expert-novice AI tool output indistinguishable to lay evaluators.

Abe's 30,000 hours of embedded struggle exist. They're invisible. They function for expert viewers (fellow illustrators, animation industry veterans, art school graduates). For general audiences watching AI art debates, reading disclosure statements, evaluating images on social media—the embedded struggle might as well not exist. It's structurally illegible.
