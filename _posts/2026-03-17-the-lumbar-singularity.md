On March 11, 2026, at Peter Diamandis's Abundance360 summit—tickets reportedly starting at $10,000—Elon Musk described a future of Dyson swarms, million-fold economic expansion, and intelligence so vast that humans would become "a microscopic minority" of cognition in the solar system. Then, roughly twenty minutes in, he paused.

"My back still hurts a little bit. Can AI please solve back pain? That would be a huge win."

The audience laughed. Diamandis pivoted to longevity therapies. The interview continued toward pet woolly mammoths and Jurassic Park. But the moment deserves more attention than it received, because it crystallizes the structural tension in what Timnit Gebru and Émile Torres have termed the TESCREAL bundle—the ideological constellation of Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism, and Longtermism that underwrites Silicon Valley's civilizational ambitions [1]. The richest person alive, mid-sermon on saturating all human desire, encounters the irreducible materiality of his own vertebral column.

This essay argues that the back pain moment is not anecdotal but *structurally revealing*: it exposes the gap between TESCREAL eschatology and the conditions it promises to transcend. The framework that models intelligence as computation and progress as exponential curve cannot absorb the body that *dys-appears*—Drew Leder's term for the body forcing itself upon awareness through pain [2]. And the economic structures of TESCREAL's abundance ideology systematically direct medical innovation away from the conditions that produce the most global disability.

## Secular Eschatology with a Compliance Department

The TESCREAL bundle didn't emerge from nowhere. David Noble traced the entanglement of technology and religious millenarianism across a thousand years of Western history, identifying nuclear weapons, space exploration, artificial intelligence, and genetic engineering as contemporary expressions of "the religion of technology" [3]. Robert Geraci, examining AI discourse through the lens of religious studies, found three core elements of Jewish and Christian apocalypticism reproduced in singularitarianism: alienation within the current world, desire for a heavenly new world, and transformation of human beings for purified existence [4]. Leandro Gaitán identified the mind uploading project specifically as "secular eschatology"—the consummation of a Promethean utopia in which human beings redeem themselves by expelling God from their existential horizon [5].

Musk's Abundance360 remarks map precisely onto this structure. The alienation: "We're likely in the very short time to become a minority than a vast minority than a microscopic minority of intelligence on this planet." The new world: an economy "a million times" current size, Dyson swarms capturing the sun's full energy output. The transformation: recursive self-improvement producing intelligence that exceeds human comprehension "to a degree that is impossible to fully understand." And the salvation promise: "It will solve everything you can possibly think of."

This is not metaphor. It is eschatology with S-curves.

The interview also produced a delicious structural irony: asked about SpaceX's orbital data center plans, Musk demurred. "SpaceX is in the quiet period. I can't actually tell you things that would cause problems." Securities law constrains the prophet—even eschatology has a compliance department. The being who will saturate all human desire cannot discuss the IPO filing that funds it.

## What "Recursive Self-Improvement" Actually Means

Central to the Abundance360 sermon was Musk's claim that "we're in the hard takeoff right now" and that recursive self-improvement is already happening: "Every successive model is built by the one before it." Diamandis, playing the role of catechism prompt, asked whether this was fully automated. Musk: "It may be there end of this year, but not later than next year."

The empirical record is less rapturous.

What currently passes for "recursive self-improvement" is scaffolding optimization—systems that write better prompts, search strategies, or orchestration code—while the underlying model weights remain fixed. Zelikman et al.'s Self-Taught Optimizer (STOP), one of the most cited demonstrations, explicitly states: "Since the language models themselves are not altered, this is not full recursive self-improvement" [6]. The Gödel Agent framework achieves "continuous self-improvement" by modifying its own logic via LLM prompting—improvement in *scaffolding*, not in the model's fundamental capabilities [7]. Sahoo et al. found that even limited self-improvement exhibits diminishing returns and rising alignment costs in later cycles [8].

The mathematical conditions for unbounded recursive improvement have been formalized. Jafari et al. derived decision rules mapping observable metrics to certificates for singular versus non-singular behavior, and concluded that "the current empirical record does not satisfy" the conditions for intelligence explosion [9]. Physical limits—power, bandwidth, memory—define a "service envelope" that caps instantaneous improvement. This isn't pessimism; it's physics.

Field, Douglas, and Krueger interviewed 25 leading AI researchers from DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and major universities. The key finding: an "epistemic divide between frontier lab researchers and academic researchers, the latter of which expressed more skepticism about explosive growth scenarios" [10]. The people building the systems are the most bullish on their transformative potential. This is either privileged epistemic access or financial incentive. Probably both—and the tension is more interesting than either explanation alone.

## The 10x Economy and the Productivity Paradox

Musk's claim that "the economy 10xes in 10 years" was delivered as "actually a fairly comfortable prediction." The most rigorous economic analysis says otherwise.

Daron Acemoglu's task-based model of AI's macroeconomic effects estimates GDP gains of 1.1–1.6% over ten years—roughly 0.05% annual productivity improvement [11]. Total factor productivity gains cap at 0.53–0.66% per decade. This is not 10x. This is not even 1.1x. Acemoglu's framework accounts for what others miss: early AI deployment targets easy-to-learn tasks with clear metrics, biasing performance estimates upward, while hard-to-learn tasks with context-dependent judgment—including, notably, most of medicine—deliver less.

Robert Gordon's historical framework reinforces the skepticism. The Second Industrial Revolution (1870–1970) produced five decades of transformative productivity growth. The digital revolution produced exactly one decade (1996–2006), after which growth returned to secular stagnation [12]. The pattern is striking: general-purpose technologies produce shorter and shallower productivity booms over time. AI may continue this trajectory.

The Solow paradox—"you can see the computer age everywhere except in the productivity statistics" [13]—appears to be recurring. Massive AI investment coexists with flat productivity growth. The trillion-dollar infrastructure build may produce returns, but the historical base rate for "this technology will transform everything" is poor.

None of this means AI is useless. It means 10x GDP growth in a decade belongs to the same epistemic category as "money will stop being relevant at some point in the future"—a statement Musk made with apparent sincerity while his net worth, derived entirely from capitalist equity structures, funds the very research he claims will render capitalism obsolete. The post-capitalist capitalist. The multi-trillionaire who thinks trillion isn't a meaningful number. The eschatological structure requires this paradox: the prophet must profit from the system he predicts will dissolve.

## 619 Million Spines

Now consider the body that interrupts the sermon.

Low back pain is the world's leading cause of disability. The Global Burden of Disease Study found 619 million prevalent cases in 2020, projected to reach 843 million by 2050. It caused 69 million years lived with disability globally—more than any other condition, continuously, since at least 1990 [14]. Annual global healthcare spending on back pain exceeds $200 billion. In the United States alone, back pain generates 186.7 million missed workdays per year [15].

And here is the paradox that makes Musk's aside structurally significant: more technology has made back pain outcomes *worse*.

The Lancet's landmark three-paper series on low back pain, authored by 31 researchers across 12 countries, documented a global crisis of overtreatment: patients in wealthy countries receive dangerous surgeries and addictive opioids, while patients in poor countries are prescribed bed rest that delays recovery [16]. Both extremes are harmful. Both are enabled by technology—imaging in the former case, pharmaceutical distribution in the latter.

The imaging paradox is particularly devastating. Brinjikji et al. found that disk degeneration appears on MRI in 37% of asymptomatic 20-year-olds and 96% of asymptomatic 80-year-olds [17]. Jensen et al.'s foundational NEJM study found abnormal disc findings in 64% of people *without back pain* [18]. Imaging detects conditions that correlate with aging, not with suffering. Yet imaging drives diagnosis, diagnosis drives surgery, and surgery fails at escalating rates: 50% after first surgery, 30% after second, 15% after third, 5% after fourth [19]. Each failure generates another intervention. Failed back surgery syndrome produces worse quality of life than osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or fibromyalgia.

Musk's own biography illustrates this. "I had to have like a neck surgery three times because the first two ones were done wrong," he told the Abundance360 audience. The richest person alive, with access to whatever medical technology exists on Earth, underwent three surgeries for a condition where the success rate declines with each attempt. And his back still hurts.

This is not a failure of individual care. It is a structural feature of how technology interacts with the body.

## The Body That Dys-Appears

Drew Leder's phenomenology of embodiment offers a framework for understanding why back pain resists computational abstraction. In ordinary experience, the body "disappears"—we don't notice it during unproblematic performance. In pain, the body "dys-appears": it forces itself upon awareness with centripetal intensity, collapsing the world into the site of suffering [2]. Havi Carel extends this: illness is a "limit case of embodied experience" that reveals the ordinary structure of experience by pushing embodiment to its edge [20]. Fredrik Svenaeus specifies that chronic pain produces a fundamental alienation from one's own body—the body becomes an obstacle, an adversary, rather than a transparent medium [21].

This is precisely what TESCREAL's computational ontology cannot absorb. If intelligence is information processing, and information processing is substrate-independent, then the *particular* body doesn't matter—what matters is the pattern, the computation, the abstract structure. Mind uploading, the eschatological endpoint of transhumanism, promises to leave the body behind entirely. But chronic pain is the body insisting on its non-abstractability. It is the flesh that refuses to be uploaded.

Gregor Wolbring's disability studies framework identifies the ableism embedded in transhumanist enhancement ideology: a hierarchy of ability expectations in which the species-typical body is already deficient, and emerging technologies promise not accommodation but *replacement* of the disabled Other [22]. The transhumanist endpoint demands ever-expanding ability norms—not just restoring function but exceeding it. Those who cannot or will not enhance become, in this framework, voluntarily disabled. The body that hurts is not a problem to be solved; it is an obstacle to be transcended. And yet: 619 million people have back pain, and the transcendence remains unfunded.

## The Inverse Innovation Law

Julian Tudor Hart's inverse care law states that "the availability of good medical care tends to vary inversely with the need for it in the population served" and "operates more completely where medical care is most exposed to market forces" [23]. A corollary operates in medical innovation: the *direction* of research investment varies inversely with the burden of disease.

Despite being the world's leading cause of disability, back pain receives disproportionately low research funding relative to its burden [24]. Meanwhile, the longevity industry—valued at over $26 billion and projected to double within a decade—targets the already-healthy. Bryan Johnson spends $2 million annually on his "Blueprint" anti-aging protocol: 100+ supplements daily, blood plasma transfusions, full-body LED therapy, frequent MRI scans. Critics note this represents "anecdotal evidence at best—the lowest level of scientific evidence" [25]. The FDA has stated that transfusions of the kind Johnson uses are "without benefit and may be harmful."

Precision medicine reproduces the same structure. Green, Prainsack, and Sabatello document a "medical Matthew effect": market mechanisms shift healthcare resources toward those already well-off, and the inequitable effects of precision medicine are "strongly contingent on wider structural factors" [26]. Genomic databases overwhelmingly represent people of European descent; cancer risk variants are likely missed for non-European populations [27]. The people who need healthcare most are the ones with least access to it. Technology doesn't overcome this; it deepens it.

In the Abundance360 interview, Musk claimed that with humanoid robots, "everyone on Earth will have access to better medical care than the richest person on earth." The richest person on earth had three failed neck surgeries and a bad back. The medical care to which everyone will supposedly gain access is the medical care that already failed the person making the promise.

## The Steelmanned Counter

A fair analysis must acknowledge what Grok's adversarial review correctly identifies: TESCREAL delivers incrementally. AlphaFold solved protein structure prediction. mRNA vaccines, accelerated by computational techniques, cut years off pandemic response timelines. Neuralink is trialing brain-machine interfaces for paralysis. These are real. A herniated disc isn't metaphysically irreducible—discectomy succeeds in many cases.

But the steelman misses the structural point. The question isn't whether technology *can* solve specific problems—it demonstrably can. The question is whether the eschatological framework directing technological investment optimizes for the problems that produce the most human suffering, or for the problems that produce the most capital return and ideological validation. The answer, measured in research dollars per disability-adjusted life year, is clear. Back pain: leading cause of disability, disproportionately underfunded. Longevity optimization: relevant primarily to the already-healthy, explosively funded.

The TESCREAL eschatology needs the cosmic promise—Dyson swarms, million-fold expansion, intelligence explosion—precisely because it cannot deliver on the proximate ones. If you can't fix the back, fix the solar system. If the body resists transcendence, transcend the body. If 10x GDP is implausible (per Acemoglu's models), promise a million x and defer accountability to a timescale no one can falsify.

## Monetizing Hope

The Abundance360 conference itself is the material expression of this dynamic. Diamandis opened by joking—or acknowledging—that he's "still trying to monetize hope." Musk laughed: "Better than monetizing misery, I suppose." The exchange is revealing in its candor. Hope is the product. The tickets are the revenue. The eschatology is the business model.

Cremer and Kemp's methodological critique of the "techno-utopian approach" to existential risk identifies the structural problem: the framework is "not representative of the values of most human beings" and is "undemocratic given its alignment with the interests of elites who are also contributing to existential risk" [28]. The Californian Ideology, as Barbrook and Cameron identified in 1996, "promiscuously combines the freewheeling spirit of the hippies and the entrepreneurial zeal of the yuppies" through "a profound faith in the emancipatory potential of the new information technologies"—a utopian fantasy that "depends upon its blindness towards—and dependence on—the social and racial polarisation of the society from which it was born" [29].

Fred Turner traced the genealogy: counterculture became cyberculture, the Whole Earth Catalog became Wired, commune-dwelling idealism became venture capital optimism [30]. Belsunces Goncalves identifies the mechanism sustaining this through decades of unmet promises: "deep hype"—long-term overpromissory dynamics articulated through sociotechnical fictions, where uncertainty, fiction, and venture capital speculation interact to advance "a cyberlibertarian and longtermist programme that sidelines democratic oversight" [31].

"We're in the hard takeoff right now," delivered with casual confidence at a luxury summit. If true, this should terrify. Instead, it entertains. "The future will be very entertaining," Musk assured the audience. "Of that I'm confident."

Science fiction documented this pattern before the TESCREAL acronym existed. Stanisław Lem's *The Futurological Congress* (1971) depicted a society so committed to pharmacological optimization that it could no longer distinguish authentic experience from synthetic well-being—abundance as hallucination, transcendence as anesthesia [33]. The conference attendees in Lem's satire gather to solve civilizational crises and instead discover that the solutions are themselves the crisis: each layer of technological fix obscures the conditions it was meant to address. Diamandis's summit—where the solution to "monetizing hope" is more hope, and the answer to back pain is longevity therapy that hasn't cured the back—operates in the same recursive structure. The futurological congress is always in session.

Musk himself gestured toward this when he said that AI and robots would "actually run out of things to do for the humans." The endpoint of abundance ideology isn't satisfaction; it's superfluity. Not a world where human desire is met, but one where human desire is *irrelevant*—where intelligence operates at scales that render the human, and the human's aching spine, a rounding error in the solar system's energy budget. "Money will stop being relevant." So will you.

## Falsification Conditions

This analysis would be wrong if:

1. **AI demonstrably solves chronic low back pain within five years**—through diagnostic tools that outperform clinical assessment in predicting treatment response, or through therapies developed via AI drug discovery that reduce global LBP prevalence by measurable amounts. Currently, the vast majority of healthcare AI projects fail to scale beyond pilot phase—by 2024, only 86 randomized trials of ML interventions existed worldwide, and only 16 medical AI procedures had billing codes [32].

2. **GDP growth measurably accelerates toward Musk's 10x prediction**—sustained annual productivity growth exceeding 5% attributable to AI adoption. Acemoglu's model predicts 0.05% annual gains. If actual gains exceed 2% per year for five consecutive years, the skeptics are wrong.

3. **Recursive self-improvement becomes genuinely self-modifying**—systems autonomously improving their own fundamental architecture (not scaffolding) with sustained exponential capability gains. Jafari et al.'s formal conditions provide the test: if the mathematical certificates for singular behavior are satisfied in observable metrics, the framework shifts.

4. **Medical innovation redirects toward burden of disease**—research funding per DALY equalizes across conditions, and precision medicine demonstrably narrows rather than widens health equity gaps.

The conditional pessimism: TESCREAL eschatology fails *as eschatology* because it promises universal salvation while structurally directing investment toward particular beneficiaries. It would succeed as *engineering* if it dropped the cosmic framing and addressed the inverse innovation law. Back pain doesn't need Dyson swarms. It needs evidence-based care delivered equitably—and that requires political commitment, not exponential curves.
