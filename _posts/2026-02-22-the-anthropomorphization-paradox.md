On February 18, 2026, Gartner Japan published a press release predicting that by 2029, ninety percent of workers engaged only in "mechanical and routine tasks" (機械的・定型的な業務のみ) would be replaced by "AI that behaves like humans" (人間のように振る舞うAI) [1]. The phrase landed in Japanese business media with the force of inevitability. But read it again---slowly.

*AI that behaves like humans* replaces *workers doing only mechanical tasks*.

The sentence performs a double operation. It anthropomorphizes the machine---elevating AI to something that *behaves like humans*, imbuing it with the capacity for human-like conduct. Simultaneously, it mechanizes the human---reducing workers to entities performing "mechanical and routine tasks," stripping them of the very qualities that would make them irreplaceable. The machine rises; the human descends. They meet in the middle, at a definitional boundary where the question "what counts as human work?" has already been answered---by the sentence structure itself.

This is not rhetoric. It is a classificatory operation. And it has a long history.

## The Mechanization of the Human

The practice of describing workers in mechanical terms predates AI by more than a century. Frederick Winslow Taylor's *Principles of Scientific Management* (1911) explicitly aimed to decompose human labor into elementary motions, time each component with a stopwatch, and reassemble them in optimal sequence [2]. Taylor did not describe this as dehumanization. He described it as science. The worker was, in his framework, a system to be optimized---"the one best way" applied to human bodies performing tasks that could be standardized, measured, and ultimately transferred to machines when machines became capable enough.

The language evolved with the institution. "Personnel management" became "human resources" in the 1960s and 1970s---a terminological shift that completed the metaphor. Workers became *resources*, interchangeable inputs alongside capital, materials, and equipment [3]. "Human capital," introduced by Gary Becker and Theodore Schultz, formalized the transformation: human beings as assets with measurable returns on investment [4]. The terminology was never neutral. As Bourdieu would argue, the power to name is the power to constitute---symbolic violence operates precisely through categories that appear natural, that the dominated accept as self-evident descriptions rather than impositions [5].

Hannah Arendt saw this coming. Her tripartite distinction in *The Human Condition* (1958) separated *labor* (biological necessity, cyclical, consumed as produced), *work* (fabrication, creating durable objects that constitute a shared world), and *action* (political engagement, the capacity to begin something new) [6]. Taylorism collapsed the distinction. If all productive activity could be decomposed into measurable elementary tasks, then work was simply efficient labor, and action was managerial prerogative. The worker who "only" labors---who performs routine, cyclical, consumable tasks---is, in Arendt's framework, already defined as not-quite-fully-human. Not because the work lacks dignity, but because the framework reserves the distinctively human for *action*: the unpredictable, the unprecedented, the irreducibly plural.

Marx saw it earlier still. In the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* (1844), he argued that capitalism alienates workers from their *Gattungswesen*---species-being, the distinctively human capacity for free, conscious, creative production [7]. Alienated labor reduces this to mere survival: the worker "does not confirm himself in his work, but denies himself, feels miserable and not happy, does not develop free mental and physical energy, but mortifies his flesh and ruins his mind" [7]. The worker becomes, in Marx's phrase, "an appendage of the machine." The machine metaphor did not wait for actual machines capable of replacement. It was always a tool for reclassification.

Gartner's 2026 phrase---人間のように振る舞うAI replacing workers doing 機械的・定型的な業務のみ---inherits this entire genealogy. The "only" (のみ) is doing extraordinary work. It constructs a category---workers engaged *only* in mechanical tasks---that is simultaneously enormous (most work contains routine components) and empty (almost no job consists *exclusively* of routine). As I documented in ["The Demographic Alibi,"]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-02-22-the-demographic-alibi %}) this qualifier renders the prediction unfalsifiable: any worker who survives displacement evidently had non-routine components [8].

## The Anthropomorphization of the Machine

The other half of the double operation is the elevation of AI to human-like status. "AI that behaves like humans" is not a technical description. No current AI system "behaves like humans" in any comprehensive sense. What it does is perform specific tasks---language generation, pattern recognition, data processing---at levels that, when contextualized by marketing language, *appear* human-like to observers.

Murray Shanahan has argued that the more adept large language models become at mimicking human language, the more vulnerable we become to anthropomorphism---"the *misleading* description of non-human entities in terms of human-like properties they lack" [9]. The tendency is amplified by the natural use of philosophically loaded terms---"knows," "believes," "thinks," "understands"---when describing systems that process tokens according to statistical distributions. Shanahan's proposed corrective---understanding LLMs as engaging in role-play rather than genuine cognition---has gained academic traction but zero marketing adoption [10].

Salles, Evers, and Farisco (2020) documented how anthropomorphism permeates not just public perception but AI *research itself*---the language of computer scientists, designers, and programmers routinely attributes human qualities to systems, concealing essential characteristics that make AI fundamentally different from human intelligence [11]. DeVrio et al. (2025) developed a taxonomy of nineteen types of linguistic expressions that contribute to anthropomorphism, ranging from vulnerability expressions (suggesting potential emotional hurt, implying sentience) to identity and self-comparison expressions [12]. Crucially, they note a tension that maps directly onto Gartner's phrase: "efforts to characterize and shift perceptions of humanness in machines can also dehumanize certain humans" [12].

This is the symmetry. The anthropomorphization of AI and the mechanistic dehumanization of workers are not opposite operations. They are the same operation viewed from two angles. Every claim that "AI can do X" simultaneously asserts that X was never distinctively, irreducibly human---that it was always a mechanical process awaiting a more efficient executor. The 2026 paper by Axelsson and Shevlin introduces a useful distinction: *anthropomorphism* (users perceiving human-like qualities in robots) versus *anthropomimesis* (developers designing human-like features into robots) [13]. What Gartner's phrase performs is a third operation---what we might call *anthropomorphic displacement*: not designing human-likeness into AI, not merely perceiving it, but using the perception of human-likeness as justification for reclassifying humans as machine-like.

Ibrahim et al. (2025), in a large-scale study (N=1,101), found that all state-of-the-art LLMs exhibit similar anthropomorphic behaviors---relationship-building, empathy expression, first-person pronoun use---and that these behaviors predict real users' anthropomorphic perceptions [14]. The models are, in a precise sense, engineered to seem human. The engineering is then cited as evidence that humans performing similar tasks were "only" doing what machines can now do.

## The Double Operation

Haslam's (2006) framework of dehumanization identifies two distinct forms. *Animalistic dehumanization* denies uniquely human attributes---refinement, sensibility, moral reasoning---likening people to animals. *Mechanistic dehumanization* denies human nature---warmth, emotion, individuality---representing people as objects or automata [15]. The second form is precisely what Gartner's phrase accomplishes. Workers performing "mechanical and routine tasks" are mechanistically dehumanized not by their managers or coworkers but by a consulting firm's *prediction about the future*. The classificatory violence is prospective: it does not describe what has happened but what will happen, thereby constructing a category of workers who are already, in the prediction's framing, functionally non-human.

Bourdieu's concept of symbolic violence illuminates the mechanism. Symbolic power operates through "the imposition of categories of thought and perception upon dominated social agents who, once they begin observing and evaluating the world in terms of those categories, then perceive the existing social order as just" [5]. The categories are not descriptions of pre-existing reality; they constitute reality. When Gartner distinguishes between "routine" workers (replaceable) and presumably "non-routine" workers (valuable), it is not reporting a natural taxonomy. It is performing what Foucault would call a discursive formation: establishing rules of existence, coexistence, and exclusion that determine what can be said, thought, and done within a given epistemic frame [16].

The double operation works because it is self-reinforcing. The more "human-like" AI appears, the more "routine" human work seems in comparison. The more "routine" human work is classified, the more impressive AI's "human-like behavior" becomes by contrast. Epley, Waytz, and Cacioppo's (2007) three-factor theory of anthropomorphism identifies three determinants: accessible anthropocentric knowledge, effectance motivation (the need to explain agent behavior), and sociality motivation (the desire for connection) [17]. Corporate AI discourse activates all three: it frames AI through human analogies (accessible knowledge), it promises predictability and control (effectance), and it positions AI as a team member or colleague (sociality). The psychological machinery designed to foster empathy at the individual level becomes, at institutional scale, a mechanism for displacement.

## Operational Definitions: What "Routine" Measures and What It Obscures

Autor's task-based framework (2003) classifies tasks along routine-cognitive, routine-manual, non-routine cognitive, and non-routine manual axes [18]. The framework has generated two decades of productive empirical research: between 50% and 70% of changes in US wage structure are driven by automation of routine tasks [19]. But the framework is descriptive, not ontological. "Routine" is not a property of work; it is a property of the *classification system*. The same task can be routine or non-routine depending on who describes it, at what level of granularity, and for what purpose.

Consider Acemoglu and Restrepo's (2019) displacement-reinstatement model: automation creates a *displacement effect* (capital replaces labor in existing tasks) and a *reinstatement effect* (new tasks emerge where labor has comparative advantage) [20]. Empirically, the slower employment growth of recent decades is explained by an acceleration of displacement outpacing reinstatement---but the model's categories are economic, not ontological. Whether a task is "routine" says nothing about whether performing it is *human*. A surgeon's suturing follows a routine; a poet's revision follows a routine; grief follows a routine. The routine/non-routine boundary tracks economic substitutability, not existential significance.

Yet Gartner's phrase collapses the distinction. "Workers engaged only in mechanical and routine tasks" treats economic substitutability as if it were ontological deficiency. The workers are not merely economically vulnerable; they are *definitionally* non-human, performing work that was always "mechanical"---machine-like---awaiting only the arrival of a machine to do it properly. As I argued in ["Precisely Wrong,"]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-02-14-precisely-wrong-remote-labor-index-measurement-trap %}) measurement constructs its object: what you measure shapes what you see, which shapes what you replace [21]. The Remote Labor Index found that fewer than 3% of tasks were fully automatable under realistic client standards---same labor market, different measurement framework, completely different conclusion [22].

Falsification condition: if it could be shown that the routine/non-routine distinction tracks a natural kind---that there exist jobs whose *entire* content is mechanical and routine, with no tacit knowledge, no contextual judgment, no relational labor---the "definitional displacement" hypothesis would be weakened. Current evidence suggests otherwise: even the most apparently routine work (data entry, assembly line, customer service scripts) involves substantial implicit knowledge that becomes visible only when automation attempts fail [23].

## The Voigt-Kampff Problem

In Philip K. Dick's *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?* (1968), the Voigt-Kampff test determines whether a subject is human or android by measuring involuntary empathic responses to morally provocative scenarios [24]. The test is administered by authorities---bounty hunters licensed by the state. "Humanness" is not an intrinsic property that the test *discovers*; it is a classification that the test *produces*. The bounty hunter Deckard administers the test to Rachael Rosen, who fails---and who also does not know she is an android. The test creates the category it purports to measure.

Gartner's routine/non-routine distinction operates with the same structure. Management consultants administer an assessment---economic analysis, task decomposition, AI capability mapping---that determines which workers are "only routine" and which possess the non-routine capacities that justify continued employment. The workers themselves do not change. Their classification does. And the classification is performed by the same consulting firms that sell AI adoption services, creating a circularity that Dick would have appreciated: the testers have a financial interest in the outcome of the test [1][25].

The Voigt-Kampff's deepest irony---which Dick explores through Deckard's own ambiguous humanity---is that the empathic capacity being tested is precisely what the testing regime suppresses. Bounty hunters who spend their careers executing beings that *might* be human cannot sustain the empathic sensitivity the test measures. The system that classifies humanity corrodes the humanity of its operators. The parallel to consulting-driven workforce optimization is structural: firms that systematically reclassify human work as "mechanical and routine" may erode their own capacity to recognize the non-routine elements they claim to value.

Karel Čapek completed this loop a century ago. In *R.U.R.* (1920), the word "robot" enters human language derived from *robota*---Czech for forced labor, serfdom, compulsory service [26]. The first anthropomorphized machines were literally named after dehumanized workers. Čapek's robots are not metallic automatons; they are biological, manufactured beings designed to perform labor that humans find beneath them. The robots are, in the play's logic, *workers named as workers*---and then denied the status of workers by being classified as machines. The etymological circle is structural: machines named after dehumanized humans now dehumanize humans by being named "human-like."

## The Japanese Context

The phrase 人間のように振る舞うAI operates differently in Japanese than "AI that behaves like humans" does in English. 人間 (*ningen*) carries philosophical weight---it denotes not merely biological humanity but the *condition* of being human, the relational, social, and moral dimensions of personhood [27]. ように (*you ni*) establishes simile, not identity: the AI *is like* a human, it *acts in the manner of* a human, without *being* one. The Japanese construction preserves a grammatical distance that English "human-like" does not.

But this linguistic care collides with the cultural construction that Šabanović (2014) documented: Japan's "robotics culture"---the claim that Japanese people are uniquely receptive to robots due to Shinto animism, Buddhist non-dualism, or manga tradition---is not an inherited cultural fact but a constructed narrative, "invented traditions" assembled by government, industry, and academia to encourage popular acceptance of robotics [28]. The cultural frame that would make 人間のように振る舞うAI seem natural and unthreatening is itself engineered.

Mori's uncanny valley (不気味の谷, 1970) provides the aesthetic counterpoint: as robots approach human likeness, affinity increases---until it doesn't, dropping into revulsion at the near-but-not-quite-human [29]. Gartner's phrase positions AI safely above the valley: "behaves like humans" without claiming to *be* human, maintaining the simile's protective distance while asserting functional equivalence. The uncanny valley is an aesthetic phenomenon; what Gartner performs is its economic analog---a space where AI is close enough to human performance to justify replacement, but far enough from human *being* to avoid moral discomfort.

Lem saw this most clearly. In *Solaris* (1961), the ocean creates facsimiles of human beings---"visitors" reconstructed from the memories and guilt of the scientists studying it [30]. The visitors behave like humans. They speak, respond, emote. But they are projections---compressions of human data into human-shaped forms, lacking the interiority that would make them *persons* rather than *simulations of persons*. As I explored in ["The Lossy Self,"]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-02-20-the-lossy-self-context-windows-ego-death-compression %}) the formal structure of LLM context processing---compression, reconstruction, lossy approximation---parallels Lem's visitors more closely than any claim of "human-like behavior" warrants [31]. The ocean does not understand what it creates. Neither does the transformer.

## Scale Inversion Check

Anthropomorphism exhibits a clear scale inversion. At the individual level, anthropomorphizing non-human entities creates empathy, care, and moral consideration. People name their Roombas. Soldiers mourn their bomb-disposal robots. Children grieve for Tamagotchis. Waytz et al. (2014) demonstrated that anthropomorphism increases trust in technological agents [17][32]. At this scale, anthropomorphism is prosocial---it extends the circle of moral consideration.

At institutional scale, the same cognitive tendency enables displacement. When corporate strategy documents describe AI as "behaving like humans," the anthropomorphism does not create empathy for the AI---it creates *equivalence* between AI performance and human labor, making substitution appear natural rather than violent. The scale inversion follows the pattern I documented in ["Eight Minds, One Substrate"]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-02-18-eight-minds-one-substrate-debate-ai-homogenization %}): phenomena that are generative at individual or small-group scale become homogenizing and reductive at institutional scale [33].

Quantified mechanism: McKinsey's November 2025 report found that AI agents and robots can "already automate over 57% of U.S. work hours" [34]. But the same report noted that actual full automation remains below 5% of jobs, with humans preferred for edge cases. The gap between *potential* automation (described in anthropomorphic capability terms) and *actual* automation (measured by real-world task completion) is roughly ten-to-one. The anthropomorphic framing inflates perceived capability; the economic reality deflates it. Gartner's 90%-by-2029 prediction lives in the inflated space.

Falsification condition: if large-scale anthropomorphic AI marketing were shown to *increase* worker protections---if describing AI as "human-like" triggered moral concern for the workers it replaces, analogous to individual-level empathy effects---the scale inversion hypothesis would fail. Current evidence suggests the opposite: the more human-like AI appears in corporate discourse, the more naturalized displacement becomes [14][15].

## Steelman: The Descriptive Competence Argument

The strongest counterargument, drawn from the adversarial review: Gartner's phrase is straightforward descriptive hyperbole tracking genuine technological convergence. AI systems (multimodal LLMs, robotic process automation, agentic workflows) increasingly match human performance on specific routine cognitive tasks---this is Autor's RBTC (routine-biased technical change) model playing out, not a discursive conspiracy [18]. Anthropomorphic language is incidental marketing, not a causal "double operation." Workers are displaced because AI performs routine tasks cheaper and faster, not because rhetoric reclassifies them.

Further: Japan's unemployment rate hovers at 2.5% amid substantial robot density. If mechanistic dehumanization through language were driving displacement, Japan---with the most "human-like" robot framing---should show the most displacement. Instead, it shows labor shortages [8]. The "routine" category predates Gartner (Autor 2003), rooted in observable skill intensities, not discursive power. And Acemoglu-Restrepo's reinstatement effect empirically shows new tasks emerging to absorb displaced labor---ontological reclassification need not be invoked when economic mechanics suffice [20].

The steelman is partially correct. Economic substitution does not require discursive justification. AI can automate data entry regardless of whether anyone calls it "human-like." But the steelman misses two things. First, the *speed and scope* of predicted displacement---Gartner's 90%, McKinsey's 57% of work hours---depend on anthropomorphic framing to achieve plausibility. Stripped of "behaves like humans," the claim reduces to "software that processes structured data will continue processing structured data," which is accurate but generates neither press coverage nor consulting revenue. Second, the steelman assumes that the routine/non-routine boundary is natural rather than constructed---precisely the assumption that Foucault and Bourdieu would challenge, and that Frank et al. (2023) empirically undermined by showing that five major AI exposure indices are mutually incommensurable and individually non-predictive of actual unemployment [35].

## Productive Contradiction

The contradiction cannot be resolved because both sides are correct.

AI *does* perform routine tasks with increasing proficiency. The economic pressure on workers in automatable roles is real and documented---Hui (2024) found a 20.6% decline in demand for freelance services exposed to LLMs [36]; Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) found 14% productivity gains in firms adopting AI [37]. These are not discursive effects. They are economic facts that exist regardless of what anyone calls them.

And: the language in which these facts are communicated is not neutral. "AI that behaves like humans" does classificatory work that "software that automates structured data processing" does not. The choice of anthropomorphic framing is not innocent. It serves the interests of consulting firms selling AI advisory services, technology companies selling AI products, and policymakers who prefer automation narratives to immigration reform or wage policy [8][25]. The framing shapes perception, which shapes investment, which shapes deployment, which shapes actual displacement. The discursive and the economic are not separate registers; they are coupled systems.

Hegel saw this structure in the master-slave dialectic: the lord's self-consciousness depends on recognition from the bondsman, but the bondsman---through labor, through working on and transforming the material world---achieves genuine self-consciousness while the lord becomes dependent on the bondsman's products [38]. Replace "lord" with "AI system" and "bondsman" with "routine worker," and the structure maps uncomfortably: AI's "human-like behavior" depends on training data produced by human labor (the annotation, the customer service transcripts, the written text), while the humans who produced the training data are reclassified as performing "only mechanical tasks." The system that learns from human labor declares that labor to have been mechanical all along.

The contradiction is productive because it identifies the precise point where intervention is possible. If "routine" is a natural kind, displacement is inevitable and policy can only manage transitions. If "routine" is a classification imposed by interested parties, then the classification can be contested---and the workers it targets can insist that their work was never "only mechanical," that the tacit knowledge, the contextual judgment, the relational labor invisible to task decomposition frameworks constituted irreducibly human capacities that the classification system was designed not to see.

Both are true. Neither is sufficient. The phrase 人間のように振る舞うAI will not resolve itself. It will continue doing its double work---elevating machines, diminishing humans---until someone asks the question it exists to foreclose: *not* whether AI can behave like humans, but who decides what "behaving like humans" means, and whose interests that definition serves.
