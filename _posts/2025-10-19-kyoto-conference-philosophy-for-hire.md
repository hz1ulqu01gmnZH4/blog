In late September 2025, approximately 300 researchers and business leaders gathered in Kyoto for the inaugural "Kyoto Conference," hosted by the Kyoto Institute of Philosophy (a joint venture between NTT and Kyoto University)[^1]. The stated mission: use philosophical perspectives to address challenges in science, technology, and industry—particularly the "rapid development" of artificial intelligence and how to prevent its "runaway" while integrating it as an "innovation driver" for business and society.

The lineup read like a who's who of continental philosophy meets Fortune 500: Markus Gabriel (Bonn University) on "moral progress in dark times," Matthias Risse (Harvard) on political philosophy, robotics engineer Hiroshi Ishiguro discussing how robots "reflect humanity," and panels featuring NTT's chairman, the president of Yomiuri Shimbun Group, and various tech executives. The conference proposed frameworks like "value pluralism," "WE-turn" thinking (shifting from individual to collective), and "ethical capitalism" as solutions to AI's destabilizing force.

What emerged wasn't so much a philosophical inquiry as a case study in what happens when ancient wisdom meets quarterly earnings—and why the instrumentalization of critical thought might be more dangerous than no philosophy at all.

## The Instrumentalization Paradox

Philosophy's traditional function is to question fundamental assumptions. Socrates annoyed Athenians by interrogating their certainties. Critical theory exposes ideology embedded in supposedly neutral systems. Continental philosophy deconstructs the very categories we use to organize knowledge.

But what happens when philosophy becomes a *product* —a service purchased by corporations seeking legitimacy for predetermined conclusions?

The Kyoto Conference illustrates this transformation. NTT, a telecommunications giant with $100+ billion in annual revenue, didn't convene philosophers to hear that their business model perpetuates extractive capitalism or that AI development concentrates power. They convened philosophers to provide intellectual cover for continued AI deployment while managing reputational risks.

This isn't unique to NTT. Recent data shows that board-level AI oversight among S&P 500 companies increased more than 84% in the past year, with 31% now disclosing board oversight of AI in 2024[^2]. Yet AI Ethics Boards showed almost flat growth since 2023—oversight increased, but *ethical* oversight stagnated. The gap between governance theater and actual ethical constraint widens.

The conference's very structure revealed the instrumentalization at work. Panels paired philosophers with CEOs not for genuine dialogue but for *legitimation through association*. When Markus Gabriel discusses "ethical capitalism" alongside the NTT chairman who funded the conference, who holds interpretive power? When "value pluralism" is proposed by an institution built on telecommunications monopoly, whose values pluralize?

Consider the philosopher's dilemma: accept corporate funding and risk co-optation, or refuse and cede the terrain entirely to management consultants. Most choose the former, reasoning that *some* philosophical input beats none. But this logic assumes philosophy retains critical autonomy once institutionalized within corporate structures. History suggests otherwise.

## The Pluralism Monopoly

One of the conference's central proposals was "value pluralism"—the idea that AI systems should accommodate multiple, potentially conflicting values rather than imposing singular ethical frameworks. Philosopher Yasuo Deguchi (Kyoto University) advocated for a "value multi-layered society" where diverse players participate, cycling between deep contemplation ("diving") and practical testing ("surfacing")[^1].

On its face, this sounds democratic. AI systems reflecting plural values rather than embedding Western, English-language, corporate biases. But who defines which values count as legitimate? Who adjudicates conflicts between incompatible values?

The conference itself was invitation-only—300 curated participants. This elite gathering proposed plurality for the masses while concentrating interpretive authority among a select few. Recent research on AI value alignment reveals the structural problem: value pluralism requires infrastructure for community-defined values, mechanisms for contestability, and transparent conflict resolution[^3]. The Kyoto Conference proposed none of this. Instead, it positioned elite consensus as the foundation for "multi-layered" values.

Moreover, the conference was organized by NTT—a company that controls over 95% of fixed phone lines in Japan through its NTT East and NTT West subsidiaries[^12]. NTT Chairman Jun Sawada explicitly called for avoiding "one-pole concentration" of AI development while representing an institution that embodies market concentration in its own sector[^1]. The irony passes unremarked.

This pattern—calling for pluralism while consolidating power—recurs throughout stakeholder capitalism discourse. The World Economic Forum champions "stakeholder capitalism" while Davos remains an exclusive gathering of billionaires and heads of state. ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) frameworks proliferate while actual environmental destruction and labor exploitation continue unabated. Critics note that stakeholder capitalism often functions as "greenwashing"—lofty promises about social responsibility without structural change, eroding trust rather than building it[^4].

The conference's proposed solution—having Western philosophers prescribe non-Western philosophy as an antidote to Western hegemony—doubles down on the contradiction. Markus Gabriel suggested that AI trained on African, Indian, Chinese, and Japanese philosophical traditions could counter the dominance of Western frameworks[^1]. But who trains these models? Who curates the datasets? Who profits from their deployment? If the same tech monopolies build "pluralistic" AI, pluralism becomes another product feature, not a challenge to power.

## The Mirror Distortion

"AI is a mirror for humanity," Markus Gabriel proposed at the conference, arguing that AI systems analyzing vast datasets can reveal "patterns in how humans feel, think, and judge," potentially helping us "understand ourselves and derive necessary values and morals"[^1].

This claim relies on a crucial equivocation: conflating *AI's training data* with *universal human nature*.

AI systems don't reflect humanity—they reflect the data they're trained on, which reflects power structures that determined what got recorded, digitized, and made available for training. As one study on machine translation shows, algorithmic systems amplify biases in training data, leading to "artificially impoverished language" through loss of lexical and morphological richness[^5]. The "mirror" doesn't show us as we are; it shows us as filtered through what Silicon Valley captured and labeled.

When Gabriel proposes AI as "ethical intelligence" that can identify our "moral blind spots," he assumes AI trained on existing moral judgments can transcend those judgments. But if AI learns ethics from a corpus saturated with capitalist ideology, surveillance capitalism's assumptions, and English-language cultural norms, it will reproduce those frames as universal. Recent research confirms this: semantics derived from language corpora contain "human-like biases"—not because AI mysteriously acquired human prejudice, but because it learned from text reflecting historical inequities[^6].

The "mirror" metaphor also obscures whose reflection appears. Large language models trained primarily on English text from Western sources don't mirror humanity—they mirror a specific subset with disproportionate digital representation. Cultural and linguistic biases embedded in AI systems aren't bugs; they're features of training datasets that over-represent dominant groups[^7].

Dave Eggers' novel *The Circle* (2013) explored this dynamic through a tech company whose motto was "Secrets are lies, sharing is caring." Transparency became a tool of control, with "all infringements of individual rights supported in the name of the common good"[^8]. The Circle's AI-driven systems didn't reveal truth—they created a hyperreality where curated data replaced lived experience, and the "mirror" reflected corporate-mediated simulacra rather than actual human complexity.

The conference's faith in AI as ethical mirror ignores Baudrillard's warning about simulation: we now live in conditions where "images, spectacles, and the play of signs replace the concepts of production and class conflict"[^9]. When AI systems analyze moral judgments scraped from the internet, they're not discovering universal ethics—they're encoding the ideological terrain where capital already dominates discourse.

## The WE-Turn as Diffused Responsibility

One of the conference's more radical proposals came from philosopher Deguchi, who advocated a "WE-turn"—shifting from individual ("I") to collective ("WE") framing across all social units[^1]. The argument: humans, AI, animals, and even ecosystems are all "equally unable" to function independently. In this shared "inability," Deguchi located a new foundation for dignity and ethics.

On the surface, this challenges liberal individualism's atomized subject. But examine the implications: if responsibility shifts from "I" to "WE," who within the "WE" bears accountability when harm occurs?

When Facebook's algorithms amplify misinformation leading to genocide in Myanmar—where a 2018 UN fact-finding mission determined Facebook had been a "useful instrument" for vilifying the Rohingya, and Amnesty International found that Facebook's algorithms "proactively amplified" anti-Rohingya content[^13]—is that a failure of "we" (humanity + AI systems + platform dynamics), or is it the specific failure of executives who chose engagement maximization over safety? The WE-turn obscures this distinction, distributing blame so thoroughly that no one entity remains accountable.

This becomes especially dangerous in corporate contexts. When NTT or other tech firms adopt "WE" framing, they can position algorithmic harms as emergent properties of human-AI-society interaction rather than as consequences of specific design choices made by specific engineers following specific corporate incentives. The "equal inability" rhetoric flattens power differentials—a Fortune 500 corporation and a precariously employed gig worker are both "unable" to function independently, but their structural positions differ radically.

Daniel Suarez's *Daemon* series (2006-2010) anticipated this problem through an AI that "hacks society rather than systems." In Suarez's novels, the Daemon questions whether democracy remains viable in a technologically advanced society, warning that if it proves unviable, "humans will serve society, not the other way around"[^10]. The shift from individual to collective mirrors this inversion—humans become inputs to a system that transcends any individual's control or understanding.

The WE-turn also echoes broader shifts in AI ethics discourse from individual fairness to "systems thinking." While recognizing AI's embeddedness in sociotechnical systems is crucial, framing everything as emergent system properties can evacuate human agency from the analysis. Structural injustice matters, but structures are instantiated through specific decisions by specific actors with specific interests{% cite berry2025synthetic %}.

## Ethics Washing as Corporate Strategy

Perhaps the most telling aspect of the Kyoto Conference was its timing and framing. As AI ethics boards show flat growth despite surging AI oversight, and as only 58% of organizations have conducted preliminary AI risk assessments despite 87% claiming they'll implement ethics policies, the gap between rhetoric and reality widens[^2].

"Ethics washing"—a term coined by analogy with "greenwashing" to denote the "self-interested adoption of appearances of ethical behavior" by technology companies[^16]—has become a recognized pattern. The conference's structure fits this precisely: gather prestigious philosophers, generate frameworks with academic credibility, produce white papers and panel discussions that can be cited as evidence of "taking ethics seriously," then continue business as usual.

Consider the conference's emphasis on AI's potential for "moral progress." Markus Gabriel argued that crisis creates opportunities for humanity to "make morally progressive choices," with AI serving as a tool for this advancement[^1]. But who decides what counts as progress? The same institutions currently deploying AI for labor suppression, surveillance expansion, and profit extraction?

The instrumentalization runs deeper than individual philosophers' intentions. Even genuinely critical thinkers, once embedded in corporate-funded conferences, face structural constraints on what can be said. Research on regulatory capture in AI policy identifies agenda-setting, advocacy, academic capture, information management, and cultural capture as key channels through which industry actors influence policy outcomes—often resulting in lack of regulation, weak regulation, or regulation that over-emphasizes industry-preferred goals[^14]. Harsh critiques of capitalism, calls for worker ownership of AI systems, or proposals to nationalize tech infrastructure don't appear in conference proceedings—not because philosophers don't hold these views, but because the institutional frame excludes them from "serious" consideration.

IBM Consulting's recommendation that companies bring in "linguistics and philosophy experts" to address AI ethics exemplifies this pattern[^2]. Philosophy becomes another specialized consultant service, purchased when needed for reputation management, discarded when inconvenient. The philosopher as compliance officer rather than critic.

This aligns with broader trends in stakeholder capitalism discourse. As the Fraser Institute notes, ESG and stakeholder frameworks have been critiqued as "roads to socialism" by defenders of pure profit maximization, while critics on the left see them as "ethics washing" that doesn't threaten capital accumulation[^11]. The result: a discourse that satisfies no one but provides cover for continuing extractive practices while appearing "responsible."

Elite gathering proposing pluralism. Monopoly advocating against monopolization. Western philosophers prescribing non-Western wisdom. AI-as-mirror ignoring the frame's distortion. Corporate-funded philosophy claiming critical autonomy. WE-turn rhetoric from institutions that profit from atomization.

Baudrillard's analysis of hyperreality becomes relevant: we've entered conditions where "it is increasingly difficult to separate representation from reality because we live in a culture of consumerism where electronic mass media maintains the 'illusion of an actuality'"[^9]. The Kyoto Conference may itself be hyperreal—a simulacrum of critical philosophical discourse that has no referent in actual philosophical critique, a simulation that preempts the real by providing a corporate-friendly substitute.

The conference's own structure enacts this. Panels paired philosophers with CEOs, ensuring that radical critiques would be tempered by "practical considerations." Proposals for "value pluralism" emerged from institutions that benefit from value concentration. Calls for "moral progress" came from frameworks that define progress as compatible with existing power structures.

This isn't necessarily conspiracy—it's how institutional capture works. Philosophy, like journalism, activism, and academic research before it, becomes another input to corporate strategy. ## Implications for Power and Infrastructure

If the conference's frameworks gain traction—and institutions like NTT certainly have the resources and networks to ensure they do—the implications extend beyond discourse into material arrangements.

"Value pluralism" as corporate product feature means AI systems with user-selectable ethical profiles, marketed as empowerment while preserving centralized control over training data, model architecture, and deployment infrastructure. The pluralism applies to interface options, not ownership structures. This matters because compute infrastructure concentrates: the three largest cloud providers (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) account for over 60% of the global cloud infrastructure market, with AI training increasingly dependent on their resources[^12].

The "WE-turn" rhetoric, if institutionalized, provides legal and philosophical cover for distributing responsibility while concentrating power. When algorithmic harms occur—discriminatory hiring, biased credit scoring, surveillance overreach—the "we're all in this together" framing obscures specific decision chains and profit motives. Labor relations shift: gig workers become "partners" in platform ecosystems while retaining zero equity or governance rights.

"Ethical capitalism" frameworks, absent structural changes to ownership or profit distribution, function primarily as reputation management. Companies can cite participation in philosophical conferences, adherence to value frameworks, and board-level oversight while continuing extraction. The material consequence: regulatory capture, where industry-friendly "ethics" discourse preempts more stringent legal constraints.

These aren't predetermined outcomes—they depend on whether workers, regulators, and civil society organizations push back. But the conference created intellectual infrastructure for a specific vision of AI governance: one where philosophy legitimates continued private control while appearing responsive to ethical concerns.

## The Conference That Didn't Happen

Throughout the Kyoto Conference proceedings, certain topics remained conspicuously absent. No discussion of worker ownership of AI systems. No proposals for public control of training data. No analysis of how AI development concentrates wealth or how automation's benefits accrue to capital while costs externalize to labor. No examination of whether "ethical capitalism" is coherent or whether the profit motive fundamentally conflicts with distributing AI's benefits equitably.

These absences aren't accidental. They mark the boundaries of permissible discourse within corporate-funded philosophy. The conference created space for debating *which* values should guide AI, but not whether *value alignment* itself is the right frame. It permitted discussion of making capitalism more ethical, but not of alternatives to capitalist organization of AI development.

Compare this to the broader landscape of AI ethics research. Work on "fairness" in machine learning typically focuses on performance disparities across demographic groups, but recent scholarship argues this paradigm is "inadequate for confronting systemic inequities that educational AI systems (re)produce"{% cite berry2025synthetic %}. The dominant fairness metrics don't challenge structural injustice—they optimize for more equitable distribution of harms within unjust systems.

The Kyoto Conference operated in similar terrain. It proposed making AI development more inclusive, more plural, more "ethical"—but always within parameters that preserve existing institutional arrangements. Philosophy became a tool for *managing* AI's disruptive effects, not for questioning whether disruption serves desirable ends or whose interests guide which technologies get built.

In this sense, the conference's most important contribution may be what it reveals about philosophy's changing social function. From Socrates as gadfly annoying Athenians to the Frankfurt School critiquing instrumental reason, philosophy historically positioned itself as external to power. But as universities corporatize, funding flows from industry, and "impact" becomes measured in citations and consultancies, philosophy risks becoming another service industry—wisdom for hire, critique for rent.
