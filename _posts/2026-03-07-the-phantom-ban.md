## The Week After Poseidon

"[Poseidon Gets Fired](/blog/2026/02/28/poseidon-gets-fired/)" was published on February 28, 2026—the day the Pentagon blacklisted Anthropic and OpenAI announced a replacement deal containing identical safety terms. That essay identified five structural mechanisms and concluded that "the god warned about the wave, the god tried to redirect the wave, the god got fired, the replacement god negotiated the same flood insurance, the wave did not notice."

The essay was not wrong. It was incomplete. The week that followed—March 1 through March 6—produced four developments that the original analysis did not anticipate, each revealing a distinct pathology of how institutional power processes dissent in wartime. This is the sequel the structural analysis demanded.

The timeline:

- **February 28**: U.S. and Israel begin strikes on Iran [1]. Claude is reportedly used in the operation [2].
- **March 2**: OpenAI amends its Pentagon deal, adding explicit anti-surveillance language substantively identical to Anthropic's original demands [3].

"Poseidon Gets Fired" covered the *event*. This essay covers the *aftershock*—and the aftershock is worse.

## The Vindication Trap

On March 2—three days after the blacklist—OpenAI published an update to its Pentagon agreement. The new language reads:

> Consistent with applicable laws, including the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution, National Security Act of 1947, FISA Act of 1978, the AI system shall not be intentionally used for domestic surveillance of U.S. persons and nationals. For the avoidance of doubt, the Department understands this limitation to prohibit deliberate tracking, surveillance, or monitoring of U.S. persons or nationals, including through the procurement or use of commercially acquired personal or identifiable information. [3]

Compare Anthropic's two redlines, the ones for which it was designated a supply chain risk to national security:

> Fully autonomous weapons systems [and] mass domestic surveillance. [6]

The substance is not merely similar. It is *identical in scope and intent*. OpenAI's amendment explicitly prohibits domestic surveillance of U.S. persons, prohibits the use of commercially acquired personal information, and affirms that DoW intelligence agencies like the NSA require a separate agreement. These are the guarantees Anthropic was blacklisted for demanding.

Altman conceded the timing: "We were genuinely trying to de-escalate things and avoid a much worse outcome, but I think it just looked opportunistic and sloppy" [8]. He also stated—publicly, on social media—that "Anthropic should not be designated as a supply chain risk" and that he hoped "the DoD offers them the same terms we've agreed to" [8]. OpenAI's own FAQ asks: "Do you think Anthropic should be designated as a 'supply chain risk'?" Answer: "No, and we have made our position on this clear to the government" [3].

This is the vindication trap. Anthropic demanded X. Was blacklisted. OpenAI got X three days later. OpenAI itself says Anthropic should not have been blacklisted. The terms were always achievable. The punishment was never about the terms.

Kafkaesque is overused. Use it anyway. In *The Trial* (1925), Josef K. is arrested for a crime never specified, mounts a defense that is absorbed by the system, and is executed by functionaries who apologize for the inconvenience [9]. The charge was never the point. The process was the punishment. Anthropic's "crime" was not its safety demands—OpenAI proved they were achievable. The crime was demanding them *publicly*, *morally*, from a position of *principled authority* rather than quiet negotiation.

The Grok-4.1 adversarial review objects: this is a false dichotomy. OpenAI's amendment could be "standard contract iteration," not vindication—Altman admitted sloppiness, and Pentagon deals evolve through negotiation cycles [10]. The objection has force. But it cuts in the wrong direction: if contract iteration is routine, then Anthropic's demands were *even more achievable* than the vindication trap claims. The amendment normalizes the terms while the blacklist punishes them. The gap between the two is the measure of how much the punishment was about ceremony rather than substance.

The formal structure: Let $$T_A$$ represent Anthropic's demanded terms and $$T_O$$ represent OpenAI's amended terms. The amendment establishes $$\|T_A - T_O\| \to 0$$. But Anthropic's cost $$C(T_A)$$ = supply chain designation + \$200M contracts + exclusion from all federal procurement, while OpenAI's cost $$C(T_O) = 0$$. The cost function is discontinuous across the *performance* variable (public moral authority vs. quiet negotiation), not the *substance* variable (the terms themselves). Being right and being punished are not contradictory. Being right *because* you are punished—that is the trap.

The scale inversion: at the firm level, vindication matters. Anthropic can cite the amendment in its legal challenge—proof that its demands were reasonable, since its competitor obtained them without penalty. At the market level, vindication is absorbed. The next company facing similar demands will note that being right cost \$200M and a supply chain designation. Vindication functions as deterrence *against* future principled stands. The precedent is not "the terms were achievable." The precedent is "demanding them publicly gets you blacklisted."

## The Phantom Ban

On March 5, CNBC reported that the U.S. military was still using Anthropic's Claude AI during the Iran campaign—the same model built by the company the President had ordered all federal agencies to "immediately cease" using, the same model whose maker was designated a supply chain risk to national security [2].

This is not hypocrisy. It is structural.

The supply chain risk designation operates in *political time*—hours from announcement to implementation, driven by executive authority and media cycles. The infrastructure dependency operates in *operational time*—months to years, driven by integration depth, classified network topology, and the non-trivial problem of replacing an AI system mid-combat. Claude was the first model deployed on the Pentagon's classified networks via Palantir's infrastructure on AWS GovCloud [11]. It had been processing intelligence at Impact Level 6 (Secret) clearance. Replacing it requires not just a different model but a different deployment architecture, new security accreditations, and retraining of every analyst who learned to work with Claude's specific capabilities.

Hegseth granted six months to phase out Claude [12]. The Iran strikes began on day one.

The ban exists. Claude is designated a threat. Claude is also processing intelligence for the military operations conducted by the government that designated it a threat. These statements are simultaneously true. The contradiction is not logical—it is temporal. Political will moves faster than operational infrastructure.

Dick understood the structure in *Ubik* (1969). Joe Chip discovers that the consumer products sustaining his reality are simultaneously decaying—the spray can labeled "Ubik" is the only thing maintaining coherence in a world falling apart [13]. The product is essential and officially toxic. The system depends on the thing it has declared dangerous. Claude is Ubik: the tool the military cannot function without, made by the company the government has declared a threat to national security.

The Grok review counters: continued use could reflect "waivers, legacy systems, or one-off uses," not infrastructure lock-in [10]. This is plausible. DoW procurement routinely includes carve-outs for "national interest" under FAR 52.204-25. But the distinction between "waiver" and "phantom ban" is semantic: both describe a system where the punishment is symbolically active and operationally void. Whether the continued use is authorized (waiver) or unauthorized (legacy inertia) does not change the structural fact that the ban's *deterrent* function targets other companies while its *operational* function is null.

Drezner's sanctions literature formalizes this pattern. In *The Sanctions Paradox* (1999), he demonstrates that economic sanctions succeed primarily through *threat*—the anticipation of sanctions alters behavior—while imposed sanctions frequently fail to achieve their stated objectives because the target has already made the commitment the sanctions were supposed to prevent [14]. The Anthropic blacklist follows this pattern exactly: the *threat* of supply chain designation (announced by Axios on February 15 [15]) was supposed to compel compliance. When it failed—when Anthropic refused—the imposed sanction could not retroactively change the decision. It could only punish the company while continuing to use its product.

The paradox deepens. Anthropic, while suing to overturn the designation, simultaneously pledged to "provide our models to the Department of War and national security community, at nominal cost and with continuing support from our engineers" [6]. Suing while serving. The company is fighting the government's authority to ban it while volunteering to provide the service the ban prohibits. This is not contradictory—it is strategically rational. Every day Claude remains operational in classified systems strengthens Anthropic's legal argument that the ban is arbitrary and capricious: the government itself demonstrates, through continued use, that Claude is not a supply chain risk. The best evidence against the ban is the ban's own non-enforcement.

## The Abdication Doctrine

On March 3, four days after announcing the Pentagon deal, Altman held an all-hands meeting with OpenAI employees. According to a partial transcript reviewed by CNBC:

> "So maybe you think the Iran strike was good and the Venezuela invasion was bad. You don't get to weigh in on that." [4]

The Pentagon, Altman explained, respects OpenAI's technical expertise, wants input about where its models are a good fit, and will allow the company to build the safety stack it deems appropriate. "But," he continued, "operational decisions rest with Secretary Hegseth" [4].

This is a precise philosophical partition. OpenAI retains *technical* moral agency (the safety stack, the three red lines, cloud-only deployment, cleared personnel in the loop). It disclaims *operational* moral agency (what the military does with the technology once deployed within those technical constraints). The company controls the how. The government controls the what. The partition is clean, defensible, and structurally novel.

It is also the oldest argument in the history of weapons manufacturing.

Lockheed Martin does not decide which countries receive F-35s. Raytheon does not select targets for Tomahawk missiles. The defense industry has operated under this partition for decades: build the tool, disclaim the use. What makes Altman's formulation distinctive is not the partition itself but the *explicitness* of its announcement to employees—and the specific context in which it was delivered. He is not merely accepting the traditional defense contractor role. He is *announcing* it, to an organization that was founded on the premise that AI safety required the *builder* to maintain control over deployment [16].

Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970) describes three responses to organizational decline: leave (exit), protest (voice), or accept (loyalty) [17]. Altman's all-hands explicitly forecloses *voice* on operational matters: "You don't get to weigh in." This leaves employees with exit or loyalty. But exit from OpenAI—surrendering equity in a company valued at over \$300 billion, leaving the frontier of AI development—is costly. Loyalty becomes the rational default not because employees agree with the partition but because the alternatives are structurally expensive.

The Grok review objects that this is "standard CEO realism" rather than novel doctrine—"employees rarely dictate geopolitics" [10]. Fair. But the objection misses the timing. Altman made this statement four days after striking a deal that replaced a competitor who was blacklisted *precisely for attempting to retain operational voice*. The message to employees is not merely "you don't get to weigh in." It is: "the last company whose people weighed in got designated a supply chain risk to national security."

And yet: voice is not fully foreclosed. Nearly 900 Google and OpenAI employees signed an open letter titled "We Will Not Be Divided" calling for limits on military AI use—approximately 800 from Google, 100 from OpenAI [18]. The letter's existence contradicts the abdication thesis—or rather, complicates it. Voice is exercised. Whether it is *effective* is a different question. Google employees forced Maven's cancellation in 2018 [19]. No employee protest has forced a military contract cancellation since. The 2018 precedent was possible because the Pentagon was not at war and had alternatives. In 2026, the Pentagon is conducting combat operations on two fronts and has just demonstrated willingness to blacklist companies that assert ethical authority.

Zamyatin staged the endpoint in *We* (1924). The One State requires citizens to undergo the "Great Operation"—a procedure that removes imagination while leaving rational capacity intact [20]. The Operation begins as voluntary and becomes compulsory—D-503 undergoes it under coercion, not choice. Altman's partition performs an analogous operation on organizational ethics: it removes the capacity for moral objection to operational outcomes while preserving the capacity for technical contribution. Employees can build the safety stack. They cannot question what the safety stack is used for. Imagination—the ability to project consequences and claim responsibility—is surgically excised. Technical excellence remains.

## The Three-Body Problem

Altman named the structural instability in his own all-hands:

> "I believe we will hopefully have the best models that will encourage the government to be willing to work with us, even if our safety stack annoys them. But there will be at least one other actor, which I assume will be xAI, which effectively will say 'We'll do whatever you want.'" [4]

Musk's xAI has agreed to deploy its models across the Pentagon's classified networks [21]. Unlike OpenAI, which negotiated three red lines and a cloud-only deployment architecture, xAI's public posture includes no stated restrictions. Hegseth granted the agency six months to phase in OpenAI and xAI models as Claude replacements [12].

The competitive dynamic is now a three-body system:

- **Anthropic**: Principled resistance. Blacklisted. Suing. Still operationally embedded.
- **OpenAI**: Negotiated compliance. Three red lines, anti-surveillance amendment, cloud-only deployment. Active contract.
- **xAI**: Maximal compliance. No publicly stated restrictions. Active contract.

Altman's own analysis is correct: the presence of a zero-restriction competitor undermines the stability of any negotiated guardrails. If the Pentagon can obtain unrestricted AI from xAI, OpenAI's safety stack becomes a competitive liability—tolerable when OpenAI has the best models, unsustainable when capability gaps close. The safety stack "annoys them." Annoyance is tolerated when alternatives are inferior. Annoyance is eliminated when alternatives are equivalent.

This is not a game-theoretic abstraction. It is Altman's stated assessment of his own competitive position. He is describing, in real time, the selection pressure that will erode the guardrails he just negotiated—and he is describing it as a *hope* that model quality will compensate. Hope is not a strategy. Hope is what you have when you recognize the structural dynamic and lack the power to change it.

The Grok review objects that "regulation stabilizes"—Biden's 2023 AI Executive Order mandated safeguards, and antitrust scrutiny constrains race-to-bottom dynamics [10]. But Biden's Executive Order was rescinded by Trump on his first day in office [22]. The current regulatory environment is one in which the Secretary of War designates safety-conscious companies as national security threats. Regulation is not stabilizing. Regulation is the selection pressure.

Lem anticipated the dynamic in *The Cyberiad* (1965). Trurl and Klapaucius—rival constructor-engineers—compete to build increasingly elaborate machines for increasingly absurd despots [23]. The stories are comedic, but the structural pattern is not: each commission requires the constructors to satisfy a client whose demands have no internal logic, only appetite. The constructors' ingenuity is not the constraint—the client's insatiability is. The Pentagon does not care about safety stacks. The Pentagon cares about unrestricted capability. The constructor who says "I won't build that" loses the commission to the constructor who will.

The formal instability: Let $$S_i$$ represent the safety investment of firm $$i$$. With $$n \geq 3$$ frontier providers and a sovereign buyer selecting on minimum restriction subject to a capability threshold $$\tau$$:

$$
\text{If } \exists \, j \text{ such that } S_j \to 0 \text{ and } \text{capability}_j \geq \tau, \text{ then } \frac{\partial V_g}{\partial S_i} < 0 \; \forall \, i \neq j
$$

The government market value of *any* firm's safety investment becomes negative when a zero-safety competitor meets the capability threshold. OpenAI's three red lines survive only as long as OpenAI's models are sufficiently superior to justify the annoyance. The moment xAI or any other zero-restriction provider reaches capability parity, the red lines become unsustainable in the government market.

This is a testable prediction. It will be wrong if: (1) xAI's maximal-compliance posture produces an operational failure or scandal that eliminates it from competition; (2) the Pentagon maintains distinct procurement tiers requiring different safety levels rather than substituting across providers; or (3) regulation reasserts guardrail requirements independent of individual company negotiation.

## The Parrhesia Paradox

On March 4, The Information published a leaked 1,600-word internal memo from Amodei to Anthropic employees [5]. Written hours after the blacklist, it is the most candid document produced by any AI CEO during the crisis—and the most damaging.

Key claims from the memo:

- OpenAI's public messaging about the deal was "mendacious" and constituted "straight up lies" [5]
- OpenAI's safeguards were "safety theater" that "mostly do not work" [5]
- OpenAI staff who accepted the framing were "gullible" [7]
- "The main reason [OpenAI] accepted [the DoD's deal] and we did not is that they cared about placating employees, and we actually cared about preventing abuses" [5]
- "The real reasons DoW and the Trump admin do not like us" included that "we haven't donated to Trump (while OpenAI/Greg have donated a lot)" [5]

The donation claim is specific and partially verifiable. OpenAI President Greg Brockman and his wife gave \$25 million to the MAGA Inc. super PAC [24]. Altman personally donated \$1 million to Trump's inauguration [25]. Anthropic made no comparable political donations. Whether donations *caused* differential treatment is unprovable from public evidence. That the correlation exists is undeniable.

Two days later, Amodei apologized. The memo had been written "within hours of a chaotic series of announcements" and did not reflect his "careful or considered views" [7]. He characterized it as reactive emotion rather than institutional position.

Foucault's late lectures define *parrhesia* as dangerous truth-telling—speech that risks punishment precisely because it is true, delivered from a position of vulnerability rather than authority [26]. The parrhesiastes tells the truth not because it is safe but because it is necessary, knowing the cost. Amodei's memo meets every criterion: frankness (the language is unguarded), truth (the claims are at minimum partially verifiable), danger (the leak damaged Anthropic's negotiating position), criticism of power (it names the political donation mechanism), and duty (a CEO informing employees about institutional threats).

The apology is the institutional response to parrhesia. The system requires retraction of the *form* (tone, emotional language, personal attacks) to domesticate the *content* (specific accusations about political donations, safety theater, mendacity). Amodei apologized for how he said it. He did not retract what he said. The content persists while the form is withdrawn—a memo that is simultaneously disowned and undeniable.

Lem staged this mechanism in *His Master's Voice* (1968), already cited in "Poseidon Gets Fired" for the TX effect parallel—but the leaked memo inverts the analogy. In the novel, Hogarth's private writings are discovered posthumously: the truth that the published scientific report could not contain [27]. The leaked memo is Hogarth's private journal: the assessment that Anthropic's public statement ("Where We Stand") could not include. The public statement says "we do not believe this action is legally sound" [6]. The private memo says "straight up lies." Both describe the same events. The private version is more informative. It is also more destructive. Parrhesia's cost is that the truth, once spoken, cannot be unspoken—only apologized for.

The Grok review dismisses the donation claim as "sour grapes from losing bid" and notes that "leaks are notoriously unreliable" [10]. This is the standard response to parrhesia: dismiss the speaker's credibility to avoid engaging the content. But the donation amounts are a matter of public FEC filings, not leaked speculation. The *causal* claim (donations determined the outcome) may be unprovable. The *correlational* fact (the company whose executives donated \$26 million received the contract; the company that donated nothing was blacklisted) is a matter of record.

## The Steelman: Genuine De-escalation

The strongest counterargument to this essay's framing is not structural. It is behavioral.

OpenAI did not merely take Anthropic's deal. OpenAI advocated *for* Anthropic. Altman publicly stated that Anthropic should not be designated a supply chain risk [8]. OpenAI's blog post says "we asked that the same terms be made available to all AI labs, and specifically that the government would try to resolve things with Anthropic" [3]. The company explicitly tried to de-escalate, publicly, at potential cost to its own competitive position.

This is not the behavior of a predatory competitor exploiting a rival's misfortune. It is the behavior of a company that recognizes the long-term danger of a government willing to blacklist AI companies for asserting safety principles—and understands that if the precedent stands, OpenAI could be next.

If the vindication trap thesis is correct (punishment is ceremonial, not substantive), then OpenAI's advocacy should be irrelevant—just another ceremony. But if OpenAI's advocacy is *genuine*—if it reflects institutional self-preservation as much as industry solidarity—then the framing needs revision. The system is not purely adversarial. Companies have agency. De-escalation can work.

Three responses:

First, the advocacy is genuine AND consistent with the vindication trap. OpenAI can sincerely want Anthropic reinstated while benefiting from Anthropic's absence. Self-interest and solidarity are not exclusive. The de-escalation serves OpenAI's long-term interest (preventing the precedent) while providing short-term competitive advantage (holding the only classified contract). That the motives are mixed does not make them insincere. It makes them human—or at least, human-adjacent.

Second, the advocacy has not worked. As of March 7, Anthropic remains designated a supply chain risk. Amodei received the formal designation letter on March 4 [6]. OpenAI's public position has not altered the government's action. Voice, even from within the system, has not changed the outcome. The advocacy is genuine evidence of intent and null evidence of structural effect.

Third—and this is the concession—if OpenAI's advocacy eventually succeeds, if Anthropic is reinstated on the same terms OpenAI negotiated, then this essay overstates the structural dynamics and understates the possibility of institutional course-correction. The test is temporal. The essay's thesis has an expiration date. Check back in six months.

## What Would Falsify This

1. **Anthropic is reinstated on terms comparable to OpenAI's.** If the Pentagon offers Anthropic the same deal it gave OpenAI—same three red lines, same anti-surveillance language—and Anthropic accepts, the vindication trap dissolves into successful negotiation. The punishment was temporary, not structural.

2. **OpenAI's safety stack constrains a military operation.** If the safety stack—cloud-only deployment, cleared personnel in the loop, automated classifiers—actually prevents the Pentagon from using OpenAI's models for autonomous weapons or domestic surveillance in a documented case, then the abdication doctrine is wrong: technical guardrails function as operational guardrails.

3. **xAI is excluded for lack of guardrails.** If the Pentagon penalizes xAI's maximal-compliance posture—requiring safety measures as a condition of continued access—then the three-body instability resolves toward regulation rather than race-to-bottom.

4. **The leaked memo's donation claim is investigated and debunked.** If independent reporting demonstrates that the procurement decision preceded and was independent of political donations, the pay-to-play thread fails.

5. **Claude is fully removed from classified systems before the Iran campaign ends.** If the phantom ban becomes an actual ban—if the military stops using Claude during active operations—then the infrastructure lock-in thesis is wrong and political will exceeds operational dependency.

None of these have occurred. All are possible.

## The Productive Contradiction

"Poseidon Gets Fired" ended with the observation that Anthropic's safety commitments produced both its \$380 billion valuation and its blacklist—the feature and the vulnerability were the same object.

The week that followed complicated this without resolving it.

OpenAI proved Anthropic's terms were achievable—and the proof changed nothing. Claude kept running in the systems that banned it—and the continued operation proved the ban was unnecessary. Altman told his employees they don't get to weigh in on how their technology is used—while negotiating the guardrails that supposedly constrain its use. Amodei told his employees the truth about what happened—and was punished for the truth-telling more than the blacklist. xAI offered the Pentagon everything with no restrictions—establishing the baseline that makes all restrictions unstable.

Each development reinforces the others. The vindication trap (terms were always achievable) explains the phantom ban (the ban was never about operational necessity). The phantom ban (Claude is still needed) explains why Anthropic sues while serving (continued use proves the ban is arbitrary). The abdication doctrine (OpenAI disclaims operational responsibility) is rational *because* of the three-body problem (xAI will do whatever the Pentagon wants). The parrhesia paradox (truth-telling is punished) is the mechanism that connects them: the system does not punish incorrect claims. It punishes *claims of moral authority* from entities it needs to remain subordinate.

The productive contradiction is temporal. Every mechanism in this essay—vindication, phantom enforcement, abdication, competitive instability, dangerous truth-telling—operates differently depending on whether you measure in weeks, months, or years. In weeks: Anthropic is blacklisted, Claude keeps running, and the crisis generates content for analysis. In months: the legal challenge resolves, the infrastructure replacement completes or stalls, and OpenAI's guardrails are tested or eroded. In years: the precedent either stands (and AI safety becomes structurally impossible in government markets) or falls (and institutional correction proves possible).

The essay is written in weeks. The thesis is testable in months. The consequences play out in years. This mismatch—between the speed of analysis and the speed of history—is itself part of the pathology. We document faster than we understand. The documentation becomes the understanding. The map replaces the territory. Again.
