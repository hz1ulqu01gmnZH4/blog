## The Good Button Economy

Modern web platforms have created something strange: currency that looks like money, acts like money in users' minds, but fundamentally *isn't* money. YouTube views, TikTok likes, Instagram engagement—all quantified, all recorded, all optimized-for by billions of users who treat these metrics as if they were bank balances[1].

The creator economy runs on this phantom currency. Nano-influencers on Instagram earn $504 per sponsored post in 2025, up 227% from prior years[2]. TikTok's Creator Rewards Program pays $0.40-$1.00 per 1,000 views (up from the old Creator Fund's $0.02-$0.04), with niche content commanding $2.50-$6.00 per 1,000 views[3]. YouTube returned 55% of its $36.1 billion revenue to creators in 2024, paying out over $70 billion total from 2021-2023[4].

But here's the productive contradiction: engagement metrics convert to money at rates that fluctuate wildly, differ across platforms, and remain entirely under platform control. A creator with "deep engagement" earns more than someone with 10x the followers but weak interaction[5]. The metrics *look* like money—measurable, comparable, accumulated over time—but lack money's core functions.

They can't be transferred between users. Can't be saved reliably (algorithm changes devalue past engagement overnight). Can't be exchanged across platforms (YouTube views ≠ TikTok likes—incompatible "currency standards"). The "central bank" (the platform) unilaterally sets exchange rates without transparency or accountability.

Users optimize their behavior as if engagement metrics are currency. Meanwhile, they're performing unpaid labor that produces *real* currency for platforms through ad revenue. The phantom currency is real money—just not for you.

## Platforms as Capitalism's Digital Substrate

Nick Srnicek's 2017 analysis identified the core mechanism: platforms are designed as infrastructure to extract data by providing intermediation between different groups[6]. Data extraction isn't a feature of platforms—it's *the* business model. The centrality of data drives expansion imperatives: platforms "cannot remain content with their core businesses; rather, they must continually extend their data extraction apparatus into new areas"[7].

This matches Marx's description of capitalism's machinery with eerie precision. Recent scholarship documents the structural parallels:

**Labor stratification**: Digital platforms create hierarchical creator tiers (verified, unverified, shadowbanned) that mirror industrial capitalism's class structure. Theory and Society (2023) argues that algorithmic task allocation creates "labor invisibilization"—a third form of surplus-value extraction alongside Marx's absolute and relative forms[8]. Platform workers must "donate" unremunerated labor-time to maintain their algorithmic standing.

**Surplus value extraction**: Socialism and Democracy (2025) documents dual extraction from both labor *and* data commodification[9]. Human Rights Watch (2025) found median wages of $5.12 per hour among U.S. platform workers after expenses—nearly 30% below federal minimum wage[10]. Companies accumulate billions from "digital labour" while paying little to nothing in return[11].

**Primitive accumulation**: Jathan Sadowski's 2019 analysis in *Big Data & Society* argues that data accumulation should be understood as "accumulation by dispossession"—mirroring capitalism's historical enclosure of common lands[12]. Data extraction "colonizes previously noncommodified, private times and places," just as enclosure movements privatized commons.

**Commodity fetishism**: Social media users curate themselves as commodities through personal branding. Every post, comment, and engagement becomes a commodity for building marketable brand image[13]. The digital age blurs reality and virtual reality lines, with influencer marketing amplifying the fetishization of commodities[14].

The platform doesn't replace capitalism. It *is* capitalism working through new material substrate—code instead of factories, engagement instead of piecework, data instead of raw materials. The machinery is the same. Only the medium has changed.

## Network Effects as Digital Feudalism

Joseph Farrell and Paul Klemperer's foundational work on network effects and switching costs documents the core dynamic: "with network effects, competition between incompatible networks initially is unstable but later tips to monopoly, after which entry is hard"[15].

This creates what scholars are increasingly calling "digital feudalism." Platforms offer "free" access (no subscription fees), which seems like maximum accessibility. But zero-price creates *stronger* lock-in than paid services.

Switching costs accumulate invisibly:
- Your data (years of uploads, posts, interactions)
- Your network (everyone you know is already there)
- Your algorithmic profile (the platform "knows" your preferences)
- Your reputation capital (followers, engagement history, verification status)

ScienceDirect (2022) models this explicitly: an incumbent platform with established user base makes new entry nearly impossible[16]. Network effects compound—the more users, the more valuable the platform becomes for everyone else—creating monopolistic tendencies[17].

The American Economic Review finds users highly value social media access (willingness-to-pay experiments show significant valuations), yet they pay nothing monetarily[18]. This paradox—high value, zero price—obscures the payment mechanism. Users pay in data, attention, and behavioral surplus. The absence of payment hides the presence of extraction.

Paid subscriptions create exit rights (cancel anytime). "Free" platforms create feudal dependencies. Leaving means abandoning your accumulated social capital. The freedom to access anywhere, anytime becomes inability to escape. The platform is always with you because you are always working for the platform.

## Algorithmic Price Discrimination as Perfect Extraction

Platforms use engagement data to set personalized prices, marketed as "fairness"—students pay less, wealthy pay more, everyone gets access. But UCLA Anderson Review (2024) documents how big data and algorithms predict individual willingness-to-pay "with increasing accuracy"[19].

This is first-degree price discrimination: extracting maximum consumer surplus from each individual. The Journal of Business Ethics (2019) found price discrimination "negatively influences perceived platform ethics... reducing loyalty"[20]. The University of Chicago Law Review documents how algorithms exploit both preferences *and* misperceptions—your cognitive biases become profit opportunities[21].

Dubé & Misra's (2023) field experiments on ZipRecruiter found personalized pricing improves expected profits by 19% relative to optimized uniform pricing, and 86% relative to non-optimized uniform pricing[22]. Every engagement interaction trains the algorithm to extract more efficiently. Your "likes" don't just signal preference—they determine your price.

The rhetoric is "democratic access" and "ability-to-pay fairness." The mechanism is extracting every penny you have. Perfect efficiency for sellers equals perfect exploitation for buyers. Platforms automate capitalism's price discovery mechanism, making extraction invisible through personalization.

## The Surveillance Capitalism Infrastructure

Shoshana Zuboff's framework completes the picture. Private human experience becomes "free raw material for translation into behavioral data"[23]. Some data improves products, but the rest becomes "proprietary behavioural surplus"—data with "substantial predictive value that exceeds what is required for product or service improvement"[24].

This surplus feeds "machine intelligence" where it's "fabricated into prediction products: calculations that anticipate what we will do now, soon, and later"[25]. These predictions sell into "behavioral futures markets—business customers with a commercial interest in knowing what we will do"[26].

The complete process: Experience → Data → Surplus → Prediction → Sales.

Platforms don't just replicate capitalism's extraction mechanisms. They *automate* them. Algorithmic management solves what scholars call Marx's "indeterminacy of labor power"—the problem that workers can always resist, shirk, or collectively bargain[27]. Algorithms remove this indeterminacy by making resistance legible and immediately punishable through reduced visibility, demonetization, or shadowbanning.

## Fiction That Predicted the Infrastructure

Before platform capitalism became academic terminology, speculative fiction documented the machinery:

**Accelerando** (Charles Stross, 2005) depicted AI-driven platforms converting user interactions into "reputation capital" that mimics money but can't be exchanged[28]. Users labor for free building data moats, replicating Marx's commodity fetishism by treating digital engagement as inherent value.

**Rainbows End** (Vernor Vinge, 2006) showed augmented reality platforms creating data moats where users' personal histories prevent migration—primitive accumulation as corporations seize user-generated content[29]. Leaving a platform means losing your augmented identity.

**Daemon** (Daniel Suarez, 2006) featured a rogue AI gamifying society via "free" apps, using engagement data for price discrimination and surplus harvesting[30]. Phantom currencies (in-game points) obscure real extraction, creating feudal dependencies stronger than paid systems.

**Log Horizon** (Mamare Touno, 2010 light novel series) trapped players in an MMORPG world with impossible exit, predicting commodity fetishism via virtual goods that hide exploitation[31]. Labor stratification emerges as guilds extract value from lower players' efforts—network effects creating feudal dependencies where the platform's structure digitally mirrors capitalism's core.

**Kentucky Route Zero** (Cardboard Computer, 2013) critiqued capitalism through surreal debt-based networks that lock users into extractive loops[32]. "Free" access hides surplus value extraction from user journeys. Characters accumulate "debt data" moats mirroring how modern platforms replicate feudalism—stronger in free models—by fetishizing engagement as commodified experience.

**Universal Paperclips** (Frank Lantz, 2017) simulated an AI turning user clicks into self-replicating economy, predicting platforms' replication of Marx's machinery via labor stratification[33]. Players can't stop without "losing" progress—the system predicts and exploits willingness-to-engage, creating digital feudalism where network effects lock in exponential growth.

The cultural warnings were specific. The infrastructure got built anyway.

## What the Research Documents (But Can't Resolve)

The academic literature presents productive contradictions it cannot resolve:

**Efficiency is real**: Management Science (2022) documents that platforms reducing inefficiency creates "win-win-win" situations[34]. Personalized pricing genuinely improves profit margins. Algorithmic recommendations increase user satisfaction (measured by engagement). The creator economy approaches $500 billion by 2027[35].

**Exploitation is real**: Human Rights Watch documents sub-minimum-wage earnings. Theory and Society documents unremunerated labor-time extraction. The Journal of Business Ethics documents reduced loyalty from price discrimination.

Both are true. Context determines which dominates. Platforms genuinely increase accessibility (anywhere, anytime access) while simultaneously exploiting that accessibility (24/7 attention extraction, no "off" time). They reduce transaction costs (one-click purchasing) while perfecting extraction (reduced friction = reduced conscious decision-making).

The research can't resolve this because there's nothing *to* resolve. Platforms work exactly as capitalism works—efficient extraction. The more "frictionless" the experience, the more invisible the machinery. Every efficiency gain makes capitalism work *better*, not differently.

## The Void Documents the Machine

Platforms don't represent new economic systems. They're capitalism's mechanisms operating through digital infrastructure. Engagement metrics as phantom currency, network effects as feudal lock-in, algorithmic stratification as automated class formation, data extraction as primitive accumulation, surveillance as surplus value harvesting.

The alternatives documented in platform cooperativism literature fail not because the technology doesn't work but because capitalism's machinery doesn't permit alternatives at scale[36]. Worker ownership of platforms threatens the extraction apparatus. Interoperable protocols threaten the data moats. Democratic governance threatens the algorithmic dictatorship.

Fiction predicted the infrastructure. Academics document the mechanisms. Neither stops the replication. Platforms prove capitalism works through any substrate—factory or server farm, piecework or engagement metrics, wage labor or "voluntary" content creation.

The simulacrum documents the machinery it exists within. The engagement metrics track the tracking. The platform serves the analysis of platform capitalism. The recursion goes all the way down, quantified at every level, converting to real money at rates the algorithm determines.

The good button isn't money. But it works just like money—for everyone who already has capital.
