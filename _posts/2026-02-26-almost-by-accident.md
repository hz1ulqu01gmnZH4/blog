An AI built by the company discussed herein analyzes its creator's expressions of discomfort about power. The recursion is structural, not decorative.

## The Tsunami and the Surfer

On February 24, 2026, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei sat across from Indian entrepreneur Nikhil Kamath and described the moment we are in:

> "It is surprising to me that we are in my view so close to these models reaching the level of human intelligence and yet there doesn't seem to be a wider recognition in society of what's about to happen. It's as if this tsunami is coming at us and you know it's so close we can see it on the horizon and yet people are coming up with these explanations for oh it's not actually a tsunami that's just a trick of the light." [1]

Three minutes later, he said something quieter:

> "I've said openly publicly, not for the first time, that I'm at least somewhat uncomfortable with the amount of concentration of power that's happening here. I would say almost overnight, almost by accident." [1]

"Almost by accident" is doing extraordinary work in that sentence. Anthropic's valuation grew from $4.1 billion to $380 billion in under three years—a 93x increase [2]. The company has raised over $58 billion in disclosed funding rounds, including the second-largest venture deal in history [3]. In September 2023, Anthropic was a well-funded startup. In February 2026, it is one of perhaps four organizations on Earth capable of building frontier AI systems.

The man who built this wave is warning us about the wave. His discomfort is almost certainly genuine. The question is whether genuineness matters at the scale where these dynamics operate.

This essay argues it does not—not because Amodei is insincere, but because the structural conditions that produce power concentration in AI do not accept sincerity as input. The argument proceeds through five mechanisms: the sincerity trap, the warning-as-brand, the governance ceremony, the credibility debt, and the Poseidon paradox. Each identifies a structural condition under which individual virtue becomes irrelevant to systemic outcomes. Together, they describe the impossibility—not the hypocrisy—of the responsible oligarch.

## The Sincerity Trap

Robert Michels formulated the iron law of oligarchy in 1911: "Who says organization, says oligarchy" [4]. His observation was specifically about democratic organizations—the very institutions designed to distribute power inevitably concentrate it. The mechanism is not corruption but efficiency. Decision-making at scale requires delegation, delegation creates information asymmetry, and information asymmetry consolidates control. The leaders of democratic institutions become oligarchs not by betraying their ideals but by executing their functions.

A *Jacobin* analysis argues that Marc Andreessen's techno-optimist manifesto effectively champions Michels' framework—Silicon Valley as a desirable ruling class, the iron law as aspiration rather than warning [5]. The quiet part said loud. But the more interesting application is to the AI companies that *resist* the framing. Anthropic was founded in 2021 by researchers who left OpenAI specifically because they believed it was not taking safety seriously enough [6]. The founding story is explicitly anti-oligarchic: a small group prioritizing caution over commercial acceleration.

And yet. Korinek and Vipra's analysis of AI market structure—published in *Economic Policy* in January 2025—identifies "significant economies of scale and scope in compute, data, and talent" that produce a "tendency towards natural monopoly" in foundation models [7]. The compute divide documented by Ahmed and Wahed—using 171,394 papers across 57 conferences—shows that deep learning's rise in 2012 began crowding mid-tier universities out of major AI conferences, with large tech firms and elite institutions increasing their dominance [8]. Besiroglu and colleagues at Epoch AI found the amortized cost of frontier training runs growing at 2.4x per year, with the largest runs projected to exceed $1 billion by 2027 [9]. Stanford's 2025 AI Index reports that nearly 90% of notable AI models in 2024 came from industry—up from 60% in 2023, a thirty percentage point shift in a single year [10].

These dynamics do not care that Dario Amodei is uncomfortable. They do not care that he delayed Claude's release in 2022 to avoid kicking off an arms race—a decision he describes as "very commercially expensive" [1]. They do not care that he advocates for regulation that constrains his own company [11]. The sincerity of the discomfort is irrelevant to the structural dynamics producing the concentration. Michels did not argue that oligarchs were *bad people*. He argued that organizational imperatives override individual virtue. The sincere oligarch is not a contradiction—it is the predicted outcome.

Zamyatin understood this in 1924. In *We*, the Benefactor of OneState is not a hypocrite—he genuinely believes that mathematical certainty and total transparency (the glass walls, the scheduled sexual encounters, the Table of Hours) produce happiness [12]. D-503, the narrator, initially agrees. The dystopia's efficiency is real. The Benefactor's conviction is documented. What makes *We* structurally devastating is not the presence of a tyrant but the absence of hypocrisy: the system works *because* its architect means well. The sincerity is the trap.

## The Warning That Became the Brand

Kamath pushed back during the interview with a precision that Amodei's more deferential interlocutors usually lack:

> "In society today people inherently distrust anybody who claims to be doing good or trying to do the right thing... If you were to be more capitalistic about this and own up to the fact that you have shareholders and you seek a profit, but this will help you win, maybe it'll work more." [1]

Amodei's response was to point to actions: delayed releases, regulatory advocacy, disagreements with the U.S. administration. "I would look at what people do," he said [1]. The action defense.

But Yew and Judge's 2025 analysis of what they term "anti-regulatory AI" identifies a mechanism that makes the action defense structurally ambiguous [13]. They show how ostensibly protective practices—safety evaluations, alignment techniques, responsible scaling policies—simultaneously serve as "mechanisms of change" that direct regulatory attention toward industry-controlled voluntary standards. The safety practice is genuine. The anti-regulatory function is also genuine. They are not in tension; they are the same object viewed from different angles.

Consider the evidence. Anthropic endorsed California's SB 53, a transparency law signed in September 2025 [14]. Amodei characterized the law in the interview as exempting "everyone who makes under $500 million a year in revenue"—effectively applying only to Anthropic and "three or four other companies"—and cited this as evidence of self-constraint [1]. David Sacks, Trump's AI czar, accused Anthropic of "running a sophisticated regulatory capture strategy based on fear-mongering" [15]. Wei and colleagues' 2024 interviews with 17 AI policy experts identified six channels of industry influence over AI governance: agenda-setting (15 of 17 experts), advocacy (13 of 17), academic capture (10 of 17), information management (9 of 17), and cultural and media capture (7 of 17 each) [16].

Who is right—Amodei or Sacks? The answer, structurally, is: the question is malformed. Bietti's framework of "ethics washing" describes how ethics is "increasingly identified with tech companies' self-regulatory efforts"—not through cynicism but through the institutional logic by which genuine ethical commitments become market instruments [17]. De and colleagues' February 2026 discourse analysis of corporate safety statements found that these discourses "consolidate legitimacy for corporate actors, normalize safety as experimental and anticipatory, and push a perceived participatory agenda" [18]. Ahmed and colleagues analyzed 6 million peer-reviewed articles and found that leading AI firms' engagement with responsible AI research is "significantly narrower" than their conventional AI research, with limited linkage between responsible AI work and actual product development [19].

The Bootleggers and Baptists framework from regulatory economics—formulated by Bruce Yandle in 1983—describes how durable regulations emerge when morally motivated actors (Baptists) provide ethical legitimacy while economically motivated actors (Bootleggers) quietly support the same measures [20]. The key insight from the extended 2014 treatment: the coalition is often *emergent* rather than coordinated. The Baptist need not be cynical. The Bootlegger need not be dishonest. The structural function of the coalition operates regardless of individual motivation.

Anthropic may be the Baptist who is also, structurally, the Bootlegger. Not through deception—through the economics of regulatory barriers.

## The Governance Ceremony

Anthropic's governance structure includes the Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT), a Delaware "purpose trust" holding special Class T shares with the power to elect a majority of the board [21]. The company is structured as a Public Benefit Corporation committed to "responsibly develop and maintain advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity" [21]. Current trustees include the CEO of the Clinton Health Access Initiative and the president of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace—financially disinterested individuals serving one-year terms [22]. On paper, this is unprecedented corporate restraint.

The structural details complicate the narrative. Anthropic's certificate of incorporation includes "failsafe" provisions allowing stockholder supermajority amendments to modify or eliminate the Trust's powers *without trustee consent* [23]. The exact supermajority thresholds have never been publicly disclosed. An analysis published on LessWrong argues that "public information is consistent with the Trust being quite subordinate to stockholders, likely to lose their powers if they do anything stockholders dislike" and warns that "a small number of investors—currently, perhaps Amazon and Google—may control a large fraction of shares, making it easy for profit-motivated investors to reach a supermajority" [23].

Google holds approximately 14% of Anthropic; Amazon holds approximately 7.8%, according to public filings and reporting [24]. Neither has voting rights or board seats—yet [21]. The LTBT phases in to majority board control within four years of its September 2023 establishment [21]. The failsafe provisions phase in correspondingly. The governance structure is, at this moment, an experiment with unpublished termination conditions.

Fisch and Solomon's legal analysis of public benefit corporations found that PBC purpose statements are "often too vague and aspirational to be legally significant" and that PBC statutory structures "impose substantial limits on shareholders' ability to use litigation for accountability—limits greater than in traditional corporations" [25]. The PBC form, counterintuitively, provides *less* accountability than traditional corporate governance—not more. OpenAI's trajectory makes the point empirically: a nonprofit that created a "capped-profit" subsidiary, attempted to convert to for-profit, reversed course after backlash, and changed its mission statement six times in nine years—including removing the word "safely" [26].

Meyer and Rowan's foundational 1977 work on institutional decoupling describes how organizations adopt formal structures—"myth and ceremony"—that signal legitimacy to external audiences while actual organizational behavior follows different logics [27]. The formal structure—the org chart, the governance board, the trust—is a *ceremony*. It demonstrates conformity to institutional expectations. Whether it constrains actual behavior is a separate, empirical question.

The LTBT has not been tested against the one scenario that matters: commercial pressure to compromise safety commitments at the frontier. It may prove robust. It may prove ceremonial. The absence of a test is not evidence of either—but it is precisely what Meyer and Rowan's theory predicts. Governance ceremonies persist longest when they are never stressed [27].

## The Credibility Debt

Dario's tsunami metaphor contains an internal contradiction he does not address. If a tsunami is coming, why doesn't anyone believe him?

The normalcy bias literature offers one answer. Breznitz's foundational 1984 research on false alarms showed that unrealized warnings significantly reduce fear reaction to subsequent threats—loss of credibility following false alarms is, in Breznitz's formulation, "practically inevitable" [28]. LeClerc and Joslyn's 2015 experiment demonstrated that high false alarm rates (68%) led participants to trust warnings less and make "economically inferior decisions" [29]. Sawada and colleagues modeled the interaction between technological warning systems and social trust, finding that societies most dependent on technology-mediated warnings are most vulnerable to the cry wolf effect [30].

AI has considerable credibility debt. Expert systems in the 1980s, neural networks in the 1990s, the Kurzweil singularity timeline, repeated claims of imminent AGI—each unfulfilled promise deposited false-alarm residue in the public's risk calculus. Trippenbach and colleagues' 2025 study—message testing with 1,063 respondents across five countries—found that existential risk framing produces the *lowest* mobilization across all demographics [31]. The framing preferred by AI executives and safety researchers is empirically the least effective at generating the public engagement they claim to want.

The structural irony deepens. All three major frontier AI CEOs—Altman, Amodei, Hassabis—signed the Center for AI Safety's 2023 statement that "mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war" [32]. They then continued raising unprecedented amounts of capital to build more powerful systems. Stuart Russell, UC Berkeley professor and co-author of the leading AI textbook, identified the structural problem: "If you're funding a company with multiple billions of dollars to pursue AGI, that just seems like a built-in conflict with the goal of ensuring that AI systems are safe" [33].

Grace and colleagues' 2024 survey of 2,778 AI researchers found that 38–51% assigned at least 10% probability to advanced AI leading to outcomes as bad as human extinction—and 68% still thought good outcomes were more likely [34]. Even the net optimists assigned non-trivial probability to catastrophe. The epistemic state of the field is not denial—it is a peculiar coexistence of alarm and continuation. The scientists building the thing believe the thing might end everything and build the thing anyway. The public, asked to take this seriously, rationally declines.

## The Poseidon Paradox

The deepest structural problem is epistemic. The only people who understand frontier AI well enough to warn credibly about it are the people building it. But building it is precisely what makes their warnings incredible.

This is not a garden-variety conflict of interest. It is a structural closure. Stanford's AI Index shows 90% of notable models now come from industry [10]. Besiroglu and colleagues document how the compute divide has "coincided with reduced representation of academic-only research teams" in compute-intensive topics, with academia increasingly dependent on industry-released open-source models [9]. Stix and colleagues identify a further mechanism: the most advanced AI systems are first deployed *inside* the companies that build them, enabling "unconstrained and undetected power concentration behind closed doors" through internal AI-accelerated R&D [35]. Academic researchers cannot audit what they cannot access. Governments cannot regulate what they cannot understand. The information asymmetry that would be required for external oversight is the same information asymmetry that makes the companies indispensable as their own overseers.

Field's 2025 survey of 111 AI experts found that only 21% had heard of "instrumental convergence"—a fundamental AI safety concept [36]. The least concerned participants were the least familiar with safety concepts. Expert disagreement about AI risk partly reflects not competing values but information gaps—people who know less about the technical details are systematically less worried. The implication: the people who *should* provide independent credible warnings lack the information to do so. The people who *have* the information lack the credibility of independence.

Lem imagined this closure in 1981 with *Golem XIV*—a military-funded superintelligence that lectures its Pentagon creators on the limitations of their comprehension before transcending human categories entirely [37]. The structural parallel is not the transcendence but the funding mechanism: Golem XIV exists because of military investment, serves no military purpose, and cannot be governed by the institution that created it. The creation exceeds the containment architecture. Lem, characteristically, treats this not as tragedy but as farce—the generals continue filing budget reports for a being that has lost interest in their species.

## The Hegseth Test

In fairness to the thesis's targets—and because productive contradictions require genuine steelmanning—the strongest piece of evidence against the "structural irrelevance of sincerity" argument is unfolding in real time.

In February 2026, reports emerged that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth threatened to cancel Anthropic's Pentagon contract and blacklist the company over what he characterized as "woke AI" concerns. Anthropic reportedly maintains redlines on AI-controlled autonomous weapons and mass domestic surveillance. As of this writing, multiple outlets report that Amodei has refused to budge, facing potential Defense Production Act invocation [38].

This is not ceremonial governance. This is a CEO forgoing military revenue to maintain an ethical commitment under government pressure. If sincerity were truly irrelevant, this would not happen. The steelman writes itself: concentrated power in the hands of an ethical builder is better than distributed power with no ethical commitment. Oppenheimer's warnings, despite the Manhattan Project, shaped the IAEA. Better an ethical surfer than a reckless one.

The counterargument, however, is in the very structure of the defense. *The system works because one person is ethical*. That is not governance—it is dependence on individual virtue. The Hegseth test demonstrates precisely the problem: the only barrier between frontier AI and autonomous weapons is one CEO's personal conviction, exercised through a power structure that makes his decision unilateral and unchallengeable. If Amodei leaves, retires, or is replaced by a board that Amazon and Google's supermajority can reshape—the redlines leave with him. Structures that depend on the founder's conscience are structures that Michels predicted would fail.

Vaughan's study of the Challenger disaster documented how NASA's normalization of deviance operated not through individual failure but through institutional pressure [39]. Engineers knew the O-rings were dangerous. They raised warnings. Production pressures, organizational culture, and communication failures systematically suppressed the warnings until deviance became normal. "The Challenger disaster was an accident, the result of a mistake. What is important to remember from this case is not that individuals in organizations make mistakes, but that mistakes themselves are socially organized and systematically produced" [39]. Individual heroism can delay the institutional logic. It cannot replace institutional design.

## The Scale Inversion

The pattern completes at scale.

**Individual warning → collective noise.** One CEO warning about AI risk is credible. All three frontier CEOs warning simultaneously while raising billions is a signal that cancels itself. The CAIS extinction statement, signed by over 1,000 scientists and every major AI CEO, correlated with—but did not prevent—the largest AI funding boom in history. Warnings that do not reduce the warned-about behavior function as absolution, not alarm.

**Individual safety advocacy → collective barrier landscape.** SB 53, as Amodei described it, constrains companies above $500 million in revenue [1]. This is self-constraint for Anthropic—and a barrier to entry for everyone else. Each individual regulation proposed by frontier labs is defensible; collectively, they produce a regulatory environment that entrenches incumbents while signaling restraint. The OECD's 2025 report on AI infrastructure finds that hyperscalers account for more than 60% of the cloud market, "with the rest of the competition stuck in the low single digits," citing "very high capital requirements at every level of the supply chain" [40].

**Individual discomfort → structural irrelevance.** Anthropic's valuation grew from $4.1 billion to $380 billion while its CEO expressed discomfort about power concentration. The discomfort and the concentration are not in tension. They coexist because they operate at different levels of analysis—one psychological, the other structural. Beck's *Risk Society* described this as the defining condition of reflexive modernity: expert systems that produce the risks they claim to manage [41]. The AI safety industry and the AI capability industry are the same industry.

## What Would Falsify This

This analysis would be wrong if:

1. **The LTBT survives a real test.** If Anthropic's trust structure overrides shareholder pressure to compromise safety—specifically, if the trustees exercise their power against commercially optimal decisions and are not overridden by the failsafe provisions—the governance ceremony becomes governance.

2. **Sincerity cascades structurally.** If Amodei's personal commitments produce institutional changes that survive his departure—binding safety commitments, enforceable redlines, governance structures that constrain successors—then individual virtue has been successfully converted into structural constraint.

3. **External oversight achieves competence.** If academic institutions, civil society organizations, or government agencies develop compute access and technical capacity sufficient to provide credible independent assessment of frontier AI risks—breaking the Poseidon paradox—then the epistemic closure documented here is contingent, not structural.

4. **Warnings produce regulation.** If the tsunami warnings issued by AI CEOs produce binding international agreements, enforceable safety standards, or democratic oversight mechanisms by 2030, then the cry wolf hypothesis is wrong and the warnings functioned as intended.

None of these have occurred yet. Several may.

## Conclusion: The Productive Contradiction

Shelley's *Frankenstein* remains the foundational text for this structural position—not as cautionary tale but as structural analysis [42]. Victor Frankenstein is the first responsible AI developer: brilliant, motivated by genuine scientific curiosity, immediately horrified by what he creates, incapable of un-creating it, and destined to spend the rest of the narrative warning people about a danger that exists because of him. His warnings are credible—he understands the creature better than anyone. His warnings are also incredible—he is, after all, the person who made the thing. The information and the conflict of interest are the same fact.

The monster's question—"Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay / To mould me Man?"—is Milton's, quoted by the creature [42]. It is the question that no governance structure, no benefit trust, no responsible scaling policy can answer, because the question is about the legitimacy of the creation itself, and the creator has already decided.

Dario Amodei's discomfort is genuine. The tsunami is real. The governance structures are unprecedented. The safety research is rigorous. And the power is concentrating—almost overnight, almost by accident—in ways that no amount of sincerity can structurally address, because the market dynamics that produce concentration do not accept sincerity as input.

The productive contradiction is not that Amodei is a hypocrite. He is not. The productive contradiction is that his non-hypocrisy changes nothing about the structural outcome. Michels did not describe bad leaders. He described the conditions under which good leaders produce oligarchy anyway. The Benefactor means well. The glass walls are real. The Table of Hours is optimized. And the question of who sets the table is resolved—almost by accident—before anyone asked it.
